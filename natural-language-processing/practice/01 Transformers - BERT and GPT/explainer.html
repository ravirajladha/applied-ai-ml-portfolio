1

The problem transformers solved

Before 2017, sequences were processed one word at a time. That was the bottleneck.

An RNN reads a sentence like a person reading with a finger on the page — word 1, then word 2, then word 3. Each step carries forward a hidden state: a summary of everything so far.

Two things break:

  • You cannot parallelise. Word 10 needs word 9's output, so a GPU with 10,000 cores sits mostly idle.
  • Long-range memory fades. To connect word 1 to word 50, information passes through 49 steps, each one multiplying the gradient by something small. It vanishes.

See the difference

Press play. Watch how many steps each architecture needs to finish reading the sentence.

The transformer's move: throw away recurrence entirely. Let every word look at every other word directly, all at the same time. The path between any two words becomes length 1 instead of length n — and since nothing waits for anything else, the whole sentence goes through the GPU in one shot.
The price: if every word compares itself to every other word, that is n² comparisons. Double the sentence length and you quadruple the cost. This is the limitation of transformers and the reason long-context models are hard.
2

Step one: text becomes tokens

A model never sees letters. It sees integers.

Text is chopped into tokens — usually pieces of words, not whole words. Each token has an ID in a fixed vocabulary (BERT has ~30,000, GPT-2 has ~50,000).

Why pieces rather than whole words? Because a fixed vocabulary can never contain every word. Sub-word tokenisation means an unseen word like tokenisation still becomes something the model understands: token + isation. Nothing is ever truly out-of-vocabulary.

Try the tokeniser

Type anything. This is a simplified sub-word splitter — real ones learn their pieces from data, but the behaviour is the same.

Rule of thumb for interviews: 1 token ≈ 0.75 English words ≈ 4 characters. Context windows, pricing and rate limits are all measured in tokens, not words.
3

Embeddings, and why position must be added back

Token IDs become vectors. But attention has no sense of order, so order is injected deliberately.

Each token ID looks up a learned embedding — a vector of, say, 768 numbers that encodes meaning. Similar words get similar vectors.

Now the subtle part. Self-attention treats its input as a set, not a sequence. If you shuffle the words, attention computes exactly the same thing. To the raw mechanism, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" are identical.

So we add a positional encoding — a second vector that depends only on the slot number — to every token embedding. Now position 1 and position 5 carry a different fingerprint even for the same word.

Positional encoding

Drag to move through positions. Each column is one dimension of the vector; the original paper used sine and cosine waves of different frequencies, so every position gets a unique pattern.

Low dimensions (left) change fast between neighbouring positions; high dimensions (right) change slowly. Together they act like the digits of a binary clock.

Interview line: "Self-attention is permutation-invariant, so positional information has to be added explicitly. The original paper used fixed sinusoids; modern models use learned embeddings or rotary encodings (RoPE)."
4

Attention, computed by hand

Four steps, three tiny tokens, two dimensions. Small enough to check the arithmetic yourself.

Here is the whole idea in one sentence: every token asks every other token "how relevant are you to me?", and then rebuilds itself as a weighted blend of the answers.

Let's do it with a 3-word sentence and vectors of size 2, so the numbers stay readable. Step through it.

The four steps

Focus token: "it". Click Next to advance one step at a time.

Why divide by √dk? Dot products grow with the number of dimensions. With d=64, raw scores can reach the hundreds — and softmax of a large number is effectively a hard max: one weight becomes 1.0 and everything else 0. The gradient dies. Dividing by √dk keeps the scores in a range where softmax stays soft and gradients keep flowing.
5

Query, Key and Value — what they actually mean

Three different projections of the same token, each with a different job.

Every token produces three vectors by multiplying its embedding with three learned weight matrices:

VectorMade byIts jobLibrary analogy
Queryx · WQWhat I am looking for The topic you ask the librarian for
Keyx · WKWhat I advertise about myself The label on each book's spine
Valuex · WVWhat I actually contribute The contents inside the book

You match your query against every key to decide how much attention each book deserves — then you take a blend of their values. The matching and the content are deliberately separate, which is what lets a token be highly relevant without dominating what gets copied.

6

Multi-head attention

One attention pattern is not enough. Run several in parallel.

A single attention layer produces one set of weights — one opinion about what relates to what. But language has many kinds of relationship at once: grammatical subject, what a pronoun refers to, which adjective modifies which noun.

So the model splits its dimensions into heads — typically 8, 12 or 16 — and runs attention separately in each, with its own WQ, WK, WV. Each head is free to specialise. The outputs are concatenated and mixed by one final matrix.

Four heads, four patterns

Switch heads and watch the attention pattern change completely for the same sentence.

Note on cost: heads do not make the layer more expensive. With 768 dimensions and 12 heads, each head works in 64 dimensions — 12 × 64 = 768. You get twelve perspectives for the price of one.

The full transformer block

Attention is only half of it. Click each part to see what it does.

A block is attention plus a small feed-forward network, with a residual connection and a normalisation around each. Stack the block 12 times and you have BERT-base; stack it 96 times and you have GPT-3.

Anatomy of a block

Tap any component.

The residual connection is not a detail. Writing y = F(x) + x means the gradient flowing backwards gets an +1 term — an unobstructed path that cannot be multiplied away. Without it, a 96-layer model would not train at all.
8

BERT vs GPT — the one difference that matters

Same block. Same attention. The entire distinction is a mask.

This is the part worth truly understanding, because it is asked constantly and most people answer it vaguely.

Both BERT and GPT are stacks of the transformer block you just saw. The difference is what each token is allowed to look at — enforced by adding −∞ to the forbidden scores before the softmax, so those weights become exactly zero.

The attention mask

Each row is a token doing the looking; each column is a token being looked at. A filled cell means "allowed". Flip between the two modes.

Look at the GPT grid: it is a triangle. Token 3 can see tokens 1, 2 and 3 — never 4 or 5. That single constraint is what makes generation possible, because at inference time the future genuinely does not exist yet.

Say this in an interview: "BERT is encoder-only and bidirectional — every token sees the full sentence in both directions, so it is built for understanding. GPT is decoder-only and causal — each token sees only what came before it, so it is built for generation. Mechanically the difference is the attention mask."

Why BERT cannot simply generate text

If every token can see every other token, then during training the model could look at the word it is meant to predict. It would learn nothing — the answer is in the input. That is exactly why BERT needs a different training objective, which is the next section.

Why GPT is bad at some understanding tasks

When GPT encodes the third word of a sentence, it has not yet read the rest. Its representation of that word is built from incomplete information. For classification or entity extraction, where the whole sentence is available anyway, throwing away the right-hand context is a real handicap.

How each one is pre-trained

Different masks force different training objectives.

BERT

Masked Language Modelling

Hide about 15% of tokens at random and make the model reconstruct them using context from both sides. It is a fill-in-the-blank exam.

GPT

Next-token prediction

Given everything so far, predict the next token. Repeat over the entire internet. It is autocomplete, taken extremely seriously.

Watch each objective run

Two different exams on the same sentence.

The mismatch nobody mentions: the [MASK] token appears during BERT's pre-training but never in real downstream data. This train/serve discrepancy is a known weakness — RoBERTa and ELECTRA were partly built to address it.

Where the field went: decoder-only won. Scaling turned out to work better on the next-token objective, and a large enough generative model can do classification just by being asked. But BERT-style encoders are far from dead — they are small, fast and cheap, and every RAG system on earth uses an encoder to produce its embeddings. If you need to embed ten million documents, you do not use GPT-4 for it.

Check yourself

If you can answer these without hesitating, you understand the lesson.

The six sentences worth memorising

  1. A transformer replaces recurrence with attention, so all positions compute in parallel and any two tokens are one step apart.
  2. Attention = softmax(QKᵀ/√dk)·V — query matches key to get weights, weights blend the values.
  3. √dk stops the softmax saturating; without it gradients vanish.
  4. Self-attention is permutation-invariant, so position must be added explicitly.
  5. BERT = encoder-only + bidirectional + masked LM → understanding. GPT = decoder-only + causal + next-token → generation.
  6. Attention costs O(n²) in sequence length — the reason long context is hard.
