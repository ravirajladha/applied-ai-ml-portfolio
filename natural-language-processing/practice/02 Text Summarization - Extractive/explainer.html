Extractive text summarization

A summarizer that reads an article and hands back the three sentences that matter. We build it from nothing: split the text, count the words, learn which words are actually informative, measure how much two sentences have in common, then let the sentences vote each other into the summary. Every number on this page is computed live by the same maths you are about to write.

Two kinds of summary

There are exactly two ways to shorten a piece of text, and they are completely different jobs.

Imagine you are handed a long article and told to cut it to three lines. You have two honest options.

Extractive  the highlighter

Pick sentences that are already there.

You read the article with a highlighter and mark the three best sentences. You write nothing of your own. The summary is a subset of the original, word for word.

Abstractive  the retelling

Write new sentences in your own words.

You read the article, close it, and tell a friend what it said. Your sentences may appear nowhere in the original. This is what ChatGPT does.

See the difference on one article

Same source text, two styles of summary. Notice the extractive one is copy-paste, while the abstractive one uses words the article never used.

This topic is extractive. It is the older, simpler, far more transparent family — pure counting and linear algebra, no training, no GPU, no downloads. It also cannot ever invent a false fact, because it is physically incapable of writing a new sentence. Topic 03 does the abstractive side, and then we put the two side by side.
One honest weakness, up front. Extractive summaries can read like a badly cut trailer — sentence 7 followed by sentence 2, with a phrase like “that flood” now referring to nothing. Watch for it; you will see it happen in the notebook.

Why a news article? News is written in a shape that suits extractive methods — the important facts are stated plainly, one per sentence. Extractive summarizers do much worse on a conversation or a novel, where the point is spread thinly across many lines and no single sentence carries it.

Step 2 — bag of words

Turning a sentence into a list of words, and throwing away the ones that carry no information.

A computer cannot compare two sentences as sentences. It needs numbers. The oldest and still most useful trick is the bag of words: forget grammar and word order, and keep only which words appeared, and how often.

“Bag” is the right image — tip the sentence into a bag and shake it. The dog bit the man and the man bit the dog become the same bag. That sounds like a fatal flaw, and for some tasks it is. For “is this sentence about the flood barrier?” it turns out not to matter much.

Two clean-up moves before bagging:

MoveWhat it doesWhy
Normalise"The Barrier!" → the barrier Lowercase and strip punctuation, so Barrier, barrier and barrier, count as one word instead of three.
Drop stopwordsthe barrier → barrier the, of, is appear in every sentence, so they tell you nothing about which sentence is special. Stopword = a word so common it is worthless as a signal.

Tokenizer

Jargon, one line each.  Token = one word after cleaning. Tokenize = chop text into tokens. Vocabulary = the sorted set of all distinct tokens in the whole document. Vector = a plain list of numbers, one slot per vocabulary word.

Step 3 — TF, term frequency

The first guess at importance: a sentence matters if it repeats the article's common words.

Term frequency is exactly what it sounds like — how many times a word appears. Usually divided by the sentence length, so a long sentence doesn't win just for being long:

tf(word, sentence) = count of word in sentence ÷ total tokens in sentence

Now the whole article becomes a grid: one row per sentence, one column per vocabulary word. This grid is called the document-term matrix, and building it is most of the work in the notebook.

The document-term matrix

Rows are sentences, columns are the article's most frequent words. Darker = higher count. Toggle between raw counts and length-normalised tf.

But TF alone is a bad judge. In this article barrier appears in five of eight sentences. A sentence scores well just for containing the article's topic word — which every sentence does. TF tells you what the article is about; it does not tell you which sentence is distinctive. That is what IDF fixes.
6

Step 4 — IDF, the rarity bonus

The one idea in this whole topic worth carrying into every other NLP task you will ever do.

Here is the insight. In an article about a flood barrier, the word barrier is useless for telling sentences apart — it's everywhere. But the word schools, appearing in exactly one sentence, is a strong signal that that sentence says something no other sentence says.

The rule: a word is informative in proportion to how rare it is across sentences. Common everywhere → near-zero weight. Appears once → high weight.

We express that with inverse document frequency. First, df(word) = document frequency = in how many sentences does the word appear at all. Then:

idf(word) = log( N ÷ df(word) )

where N is the number of sentences. The log is there to soften the curve — without it, a word in 1 sentence out of 100 would be rated a hundred times more valuable than a word in all 100, which is too aggressive.

Feel the curve

Drag: in how many of our 8 sentences does the word appear? Watch what idf does.

Multiply the two together and you get the famous quantity:

tf-idf(word, sentence) = tf(word, sentence) × idf(word)

Read it in plain English: “this word is frequent here, and rare elsewhere.” That is the definition of a word worth paying attention to.

Top of the list = the words that actually distinguish one sentence from another.

Method 1 — score each sentence, take the top three

Our first working summarizer. About four lines of real code.

We now have a tf-idf weight for every word in every sentence. The simplest possible summarizer:

  1. Score each sentence = sum of the tf-idf weights of its words.
  2. Sort. Take the top k.
  3. Put them back in original order, so the summary still reads forwards.
No length correction is needed at step 1, and it is worth seeing why: tf was already divided by sentence length back in section 5. A 30-word sentence's tf values are individually smaller, so summing them does not automatically beat a short sentence. The normalisation was done once, early, and it keeps paying off.

The flaw in this method. Each sentence is judged entirely on its own, in isolation. Nothing stops the top 3 from being three sentences that all say the same thing. And a sentence packed with one-off rare words — a stray quote, a list of names — scores highly while being a terrible summary. We need a method where sentences are judged in relation to each other.

And this is not hypothetical: look at the three sentences it picked above, then at the article in section 2. It took the technical detail and left out who approved what. In section 11 we measure exactly how badly that does — it loses to taking the first three sentences blind.

Step 5 — how similar are two sentences?

Cosine similarity: the angle between two sentences.

Each sentence is now a vector — a list of numbers. To ask “how alike are these two sentences?” we ask a geometric question: what is the angle between their two vectors?

The analogy: think of each sentence as an arrow pointing somewhere in a room. Two sentences about the same thing point in nearly the same direction, whatever their length. Two unrelated sentences point at right angles. We care about direction, not length — which is exactly why we use the angle and not the distance. A one-line sentence and a long paragraph on the same topic should count as similar.

cos(A, B) = (A · B) ÷ (‖A‖ × ‖B‖)

PieceNameIn plain words
A · Bdot product Multiply the two vectors slot by slot and add it all up. Big when both vectors are large in the same slots — i.e. when they share words.
‖A‖norm / length The arrow's length: square everything, add, square-root. Dividing by it cancels out sentence length.
costhe result 1 = same direction (identical wording). 0 = no words in common at all. Never negative here, since word counts are never negative.

This matrix is the bridge. It turns our article into a graph: 8 nodes, one per sentence, with an edge between every pair, weighted by how similar they are. And once you have a graph, you can ask the question Google asked about the web — which node is most important?

Method 2 — TextRank

Let the sentences vote for each other. This is PageRank, the algorithm that built Google, applied to sentences instead of web pages.

Forget maths for a moment. Picture a room of eight people, each holding the same amount of money. Everyone splits their money among the people they consider similar to themselves, and hands it over. Then everyone does it again with the money they now hold. And again.

After enough rounds, the money stops sloshing around and settles. Who ends up rich? Not the person with the most friends, exactly — the person whose friends are themselves rich. Importance flows.

The circular idea that makes it work: a sentence is important if important sentences are similar to it. That definition refers to itself, which sounds broken — but if you just keep passing the money around, it converges to one stable answer. That repeated passing is called power iteration, and it is the whole algorithm.

One round, written out:

score(i) = (1 − d)/N  +  d × ∑j [ sim(j,i) / ∑k sim(j,k) ] × score(j)

SymbolMeaning
score(j)How much money sentence j is holding right now.
sim(j,i) / ∑ sim(j,k)The share of j's money that goes to i — j's similarity to i, as a fraction of j's total similarity to everyone. Each row of the matrix is divided by its own sum so the shares add to 1. Nothing is created or destroyed.
dDamping factor, conventionally 0.85. Everyone keeps handing on 85% of their money, but 15% is collected and redistributed equally to all. Without it, money can get trapped in a little clique of sentences and everyone else starves.
(1−d)/NThat equal redistribution — everyone's guaranteed basic income.

Watch it converge

Everyone starts with 1/8 = 0.125. Press One round and watch the money move. Notice how fast it stops changing — by round 5 or so the ranking is frozen.

Why this beats plain tf-idf. TextRank rewards a sentence for being central — connected to the article's main thread. A sentence full of rare one-off words now scores low, because nothing else in the article resembles it, so nobody sends it any money. That is usually the right call, and it is the opposite of what tf-idf does.
Where the name comes from. TextRank was published in 2004 by Mihalcea and Tarau, taking Page and Brin's PageRank and swapping web pages for sentences and hyperlinks for similarity. If you can explain PageRank you can explain TextRank; they are the same algorithm on a different graph.

Building the final summary

Three methods, side by side, with the knobs exposed.

You now have three ways to pick sentences. The third is the baseline you must always beat:

MethodHow it picksIts weakness
Lead-kJust take the first k sentences. No maths at all. None, which is the embarrassing part — on news it is a very strong baseline, because journalists are trained to front-load the facts.
TF-IDFHighest sum of tf-idf weights. Judges sentences in isolation; can pick three near-duplicates.
TextRankHighest converged PageRank score. Biased toward the middle of the topic; can miss a crucial but oddly-worded fact.

The redundancy problem, and MMR

If your top two sentences are near-duplicates, the second one is wasted. The standard fix is Maximal Marginal Relevance — pick sentences one at a time, and at each step choose the sentence that is high-scoring and unlike what you have already picked:

pick = argmax [ λ × score(i) − (1−λ) × max similarity to anything already picked ]

λ = 1 ignores redundancy entirely; λ = 0.5 weighs novelty as much as importance. It is a greedy algorithm — it commits to the best-looking choice at each step and never reconsiders. This is one of the notebook's optional TODOs.

Is it any good? ROUGE

The standard way to score a summary against a human-written one.

You have three summaries. Which is best? “It looks fine” is not a measurement. So: get a human to write the ideal summary (the reference), then measure how much of it your summary recovered.

ROUGE stands for Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation, and you can safely forget that. What it does is count overlapping word sequences:

NameCounts overlapping…Catches
ROUGE-1single words (unigrams)Did you mention the right things?
ROUGE-2adjacent word pairs (bigrams)Did you phrase them the right way? Much harsher.
ROUGE-Lthe longest common subsequenceWord order, without demanding the words be adjacent.

For each, three numbers — and the distinction matters more than the formulas:

NumberQuestion it answersGamed by
RecallHow much of the reference did I cover? Submitting the entire article — recall 1.0, useless summary.
PrecisionHow much of what I wrote was actually wanted? Submitting one safe word.
F1The harmonic mean of the two — the honest single number. Much harder to game, which is why it is the one people report.

ROUGE's blind spot, and it is a big one. It is pure word matching. Write a summary that is perfect but uses synonyms — “flood defence” for “flood barrier” — and ROUGE scores you near zero. Write a fluent lie using the right words and it scores you well. Everyone in the field knows this; it is still the standard, because the alternatives are worse or need a human. This bites much harder in topic 03, where the model is allowed to choose its own words.
