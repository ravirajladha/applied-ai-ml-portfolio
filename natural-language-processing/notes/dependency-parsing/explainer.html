Dependency Parsing — join the words with arrows

A friendly, click-around first look at the Arc-Eager parser. No maths to start — read, hover, and press the buttons.

★The one idea to hold onto

Last session (Parsing) we drew a family tree of phrases — words grouped into NP, VP, and so on. Dependency parsing draws a different kind of picture: forget the phrase boxes, just draw an arrow from each word to the one word it depends on. Every arrow means "this word is the boss of that word."

He sent her a letter . └────┘ each arrow: head → dependent sent →(nsubj) He sent is the boss of "He" (who did the sending?) sent →(iobj) her sent is the boss of "her" (sent to whom?) sent →(dobj) letter sent is the boss of "letter" (sent what?) letter →(det) a letter is the boss of "a" ROOT →(root) sent the whole sentence hangs off the verb "sent"
Analogy — an org chart. A company has one boss (the ROOT). Everyone else reports to exactly one manager. Dependency parsing is drawing that org chart for a sentence: who reports to whom, and with what job title (the relation label, like nsubj = "is the subject of").

Two rules that make it a tree: (1) every word has exactly one head (one manager), and (2) there's a single artificial ROOT at the top so even the main verb has a boss.

1Two ways to see structure (last week vs this week)

Constituency (Session 9)

Groups words into nested phrases (NP, VP, PP). Answers "which words form a chunk?" Internal nodes are phrase labels; words sit at the bottom.

Dependency (Session 10)

Links word → word directly. Answers "which word governs which?" No phrase boxes — every node is a real word, joined by labelled arrows.

Same sentence, two lenses. Dependency is popular for extracting who-did-what-to-whom (subject → verb → object) because that relationship is drawn as one direct arrow.

The vocabulary (define once, use everywhere)

head the boss word (parent)  ·  dependent the word that reports to it (child)  ·  ROOT fake top node so the main verb has a head

2The trick: don't guess the whole tree — use a little machine

Trying every possible tree is far too slow. Instead a transition-based parser reads the sentence left to right and builds the tree one small action at a time — like a tiny state machine. Its whole memory is just three things (together called the configuration):

🥞 Stack

Words we're still working on. Starts as just [ROOT]. The top (right end) is the one we act on.

📥 Buffer

Words not read yet, in order. Starts as the whole sentence. The front (left end) is next up.

🔗 Arcs

The arrows committed so far. Starts empty and grows as the parser makes decisions. When the buffer is empty, we're done.
Analogy — a checkout line. The buffer is the queue of customers waiting. The stack is the small counter where you handle people who need more attention. You pull the next customer off the queue, deal with them (maybe link them to someone), and set them down or send them away — until the queue is empty.

3The four moves (Arc-Eager)

At every step the parser looks at just two words: s = top of the stack, and b = front of the buffer. It then picks one of four moves:

SHIFT

Move b from the buffer onto the stack. Meaning: "not ready to link this yet — read it in."

LEFT-ARC(l)

Draw arrow b → s (buffer word is boss of stack top), then pop s. A left dependent is attached and immediately removed.

RIGHT-ARC(l)

Draw arrow s → b (stack top is boss of buffer word), then push b onto the stack. It's kept, because it might collect its own children later.

REDUCE

Pop s off the stack — "I'm finished with this word." Only allowed once s already has a head.
Why "eager"? The moment a right-hand dependent appears, RIGHT-ARC attaches it straight away — before its own children are known. Later, REDUCE clears it out. That eagerness is what makes the parser fast and incremental.

Big picture: SHIFT / RIGHT-ARC put words onto the stack; LEFT-ARC / REDUCE take words off. A sentence of n words is done in about 2n moves — so parsing is linear time, O(n).

5Arc-Eager vs Arc-Standard (the two cousins)

Both are transition-based and linear-time; they differ in which two words they act on and when they attach right-hand dependents.

Arc-StandardArc-Eager (this session)
Moves3 (SHIFT, LEFT-ARC, RIGHT-ARC)4 (adds REDUCE)
Looks attop two stack wordsstack top + buffer front
Right childrenattached late (after subtree built)attached eagerly, then REDUCE later
Stylebottom-up & lazyeager & incremental

Mnemonic: arc-standard waits until a word's whole family is ready; arc-eager grabs the arrow the instant it can, and cleans up with REDUCE afterwards.

6Where this shows up in the real world

spaCy

Its fast dependency parser is a neural version of exactly this transition machine — linear-time, incremental, production-ready.

Information extraction

Pulling subject → verb → object triples straight off the arrows: relation extraction, QA, grammar checking.
Honest note. Big language models pick up syntax implicitly, so a separate "parse the sentence" step is used less than it used to be. It still shines for interpretability, auditable rule-based extraction, low-resource languages, and lightweight CPU-only pipelines.

760-second recap

Dependency tree

Arrows head → dependent; every word has one head, all under ROOT.

Configuration

Three things: Stack, Buffer, Arcs.

Four moves

SHIFT, LEFT-ARC, RIGHT-ARC, REDUCE.

Oracle order

LEFT-ARC → RIGHT-ARC → REDUCE → SHIFT (first that fits wins).

Speed

~2n moves for n words → O(n), projective trees.

Eager

Attach right children early, then REDUCE to tidy up.

🐍How to not drown in the Python

The notebook looks like a wall of code, but you do not memorise it. The whole parser is just the little machine you watched above, written down. Here's the calm way in:

  1. Run first, understand second. Open the notebook, press Shift+Enter on each cell top to bottom. Watching the printed trace appear teaches you half of it. You can always re-run.
  2. Map code back to the picture. The stack, buffer, and arcs in the code are the exact three lanes from step 4. When a cell prints a step, find that step above.
  3. Learn 5 building blocks — that's 90% of this notebook:

    list — a row of things

    stack = ["ROOT"] · stack[-1] is the top · .append(x) pushes · .pop() removes the top

    dict — a lookup table

    head = {} · head["He"] = "sent" records an arrow

    for — do it for each

    for step in trace: repeats the block once per step

    if / elif — choose a move

    Exactly the four-way choice: if action=="SHIFT": …

    def — a named recipe

    def shift(): … — one move = one small function

    print() — show me

    Sprinkle it anywhere to see what the stack/buffer hold right now.
  4. Poke it. Change the sentence or swap one label and re-run. Watching the trace change is how it clicks — nothing breaks permanently.
  5. Don't rush. One section per sitting is a win. You've got the matching basic flow notebook, the full from-scratch parser, and the README alongside.
Mindset: you're learning to read code fluently long before you write it — like understanding a language before you speak it. Reading + running + tweaking, in small doses, is the whole game.
