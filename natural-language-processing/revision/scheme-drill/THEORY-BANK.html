1 · Module 2 — Vector Semantics

Scheme says Problem, so a sum is likely — but these definitions are what the sum is built on, and they buy the "explain your answer" marks.

Q1.1 What is the distributional hypothesis? [1 mark]
  • Words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings.
  • Firth's line: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps."
  • Consequence: meaning can be learned from raw text alone, with no dictionary — just by counting which words appear near which.
Q1.2 Why TF-IDF instead of raw frequency counts? [2 marks]
  • Raw counts are dominated by words like the, of, is — frequent everywhere, so they carry no discriminating information.
  • TF (term frequency) rewards a word for being common in this document.
  • IDF (inverse document frequency) penalises it for being common across all documents: IDF = log(N / df).
  • The product is high only for words frequent here but rare elsewhere — exactly the words that characterise the document.
Q1.3 Why cosine similarity rather than Euclidean distance? [2 marks]
  • Cosine measures the angle between vectors, ignoring their length.
  • Document length varies enormously; a long document has larger counts for every word, so Euclidean distance would call it "far" from a short document on the same topic.
  • Cosine normalises that away: cos(a,b) = (a·b) / (|a| × |b|), bounded in [-1, 1] — and in [0, 1] for non-negative count vectors.
  • 1 = same direction = same topic. 0 = unrelated.
Q1.4 Compare sparse and dense vector representations. [2 marks]
Sparse (TF-IDF, PPMI) Dense (Word2Vec, GloVe)
Length size of the vocabulary — 20,000+ 50–300
Contents mostly zeros all cells non-zero
Built by counting predicting / learning
Interpretable? yes — each cell is a word no — cells are latent features
Generalises? no — car and automobile are unrelated dimensions yes — similar words land near each other
Q1.5 What is PPMI and why the "positive" part? [2 marks]
  • PMI = log₂( P(w,c) / (P(w) × P(c)) ) — how much more often word w and context c co-occur than chance would predict.
  • PPMI = max(0, PMI) — negative values are clipped to zero.
  • Why clip: a negative PMI claims two words co-occur less than chance. To estimate that reliably you would need a colossal corpus, so the number is unreliable noise. Better to say "no evidence of association" (0).

2 · Module 3 — Language Models & Neural LM

Q2.1 What is the Markov assumption in an n-gram model? [1 mark]
  • The probability of the next word depends only on the previous n−1 words, not on the whole history.
  • Bigram: P(wn | w1 … wn-1) ≈ P(wn | wn-1).
  • It is an approximation made for tractability — without it we would need counts for every possible sentence prefix, which never occur often enough to count reliably.
Q2.2 Why is smoothing necessary? What does Laplace smoothing do? [2 marks]
  • The zero-probability problem: any bigram unseen in training gets probability 0, which makes the whole sentence probability 0 and perplexity infinite — even if only one word is new.
  • Laplace (add-one): pretend every bigram was seen one extra time. P(w2|w1) = (count(w1 w2) + 1) / (count(w1) + V)
  • The trap: the denominator adds V, the vocabulary size — not 1 — because you added 1 to each of V possible continuations.
  • Cost: it steals a lot of probability mass from seen events. That is why backoff, interpolation and Kneser-Ney exist.
Q2.3 What does perplexity measure? Is high or low better? [2 marks]
  • Perplexity = the inverse probability of the test set, normalised by length: PP(W) = P(w1…wN)^(−1/N).
  • Intuition: the average number of equally-likely words the model is choosing between at each step. Perplexity 100 means it is as confused as if guessing uniformly among 100 words.
  • Lower is better. Lower perplexity = higher probability assigned to real text = better model.
  • Only comparable between models with the same vocabulary.
Q2.4 Give three advantages of a neural LM over an n-gram LM. [3 marks]
  1. Generalisation via embeddings. dog and cat have similar vectors, so a pattern learned for one transfers to the other. An n-gram model treats them as unrelated symbols.
  2. No sparsity / no zero counts. Every word gets non-zero probability from the softmax — no smoothing needed.
  3. Longer, cheaper context. N-gram tables grow as V^n; a neural LM's parameters grow roughly linearly, so it can condition on more context.
  4. (bonus) Parameters are shared across contexts instead of one stored count per context.
Q2.5 What does the softmax layer do, and why exponentiate? [2 marks]
  • Softmax turns unbounded real-valued scores into a probability distribution: P(i) = e^(zi) / Σⱼ e^(zj).
  • Why exponentiate: it forces every value positive (raw scores can be negative) and amplifies the leading score, so the model expresses confidence rather than a flat guess.
  • The output is guaranteed to sum to 1 — your free correctness check.
Q2.6 Why is a non-linear activation needed at all? [1 mark]
  • Without it, stacking layers is just multiplying matrices, and the composition of linear maps is still one linear map. The network could never learn anything a single layer couldn't — the classic XOR failure.
  • The non-linearity is what makes depth worth having.

3 · Module 5 — POS Tagging

Q3.1 What is POS tagging and why is it hard? [2 marks]
  • Assigning each word in a sentence its part of speech (noun, verb, determiner …) from a fixed tagset.
  • Hard because of ambiguity: the same word takes different tags in different contexts. book → noun in "read a book", verb in "book a flight".
  • Around 85% of word types are unambiguous — but the ambiguous ones are the common words, so a large share of tokens in running text are ambiguous.
  • Also: unknown words never seen in training must still be tagged.
Q3.2 Open class vs closed class words. [2 marks]
Open class Closed class
Members nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs determiners, prepositions, pronouns, conjunctions, auxiliaries
New members yes — new nouns/verbs coined constantly (to google) no — a fixed, small, stable list
Size tens of thousands a few dozen to a few hundred
Carry content meaning grammatical function
Unknown words almost always land here (usually nouns) never
Q3.3 What is the most-frequent-tag baseline, and why does it matter? [2 marks]
  • Tag every word with the tag it carried most often in training; tag unknown words as a noun (usually proper noun).
  • It scores about 90% accuracy — surprisingly high, because most tokens are unambiguous.
  • Why it matters: it is the number any real tagger must beat. A model at 88% is worse than doing nothing clever. Good HMM and neural taggers reach ~97%.
Q3.4 Name three approaches to POS tagging with one strength each. [3 marks]
  1. Rule-based — hand-written linguistic rules. Transparent and debuggable; needs no training data.
  2. Stochastic / HMM — learns transition and emission probabilities from a tagged corpus. Fast, principled, no feature engineering.
  3. Neural (Bi-LSTM / BERT) — learns contextual representations. Highest accuracy; handles unknown words through sub-word and character information.

4 · Module 6 — Hidden Markov Models

Q4.1 What makes an HMM "hidden"? Define its components. [2 marks]
  • The states (the POS tags) are not observed — you only see the emissions (the words), and must infer the state sequence from them.
  • Components:
    • Q — the set of hidden states (the tagset)
    • A — transition probabilities P(tag_i | tag_i-1)
    • B — emission probabilities P(word | tag)
    • π — initial / start probabilities
Q4.2 State the two independence assumptions an HMM makes. [2 marks]
  1. Markov assumption — the current state depends only on the immediately previous state, not on the whole history.
  2. Output independence — the observed word depends only on the state that produced it, not on neighbouring words or states.

Both are clearly false of real language, but they make exact inference cheap — which is the whole point.

Q4.3 The three fundamental problems of an HMM, and the algorithm for each. [3 marks]
# Problem Question it answers Algorithm
1 Likelihood How probable is this observation sequence? Forward
2 Decoding What is the most likely hidden state sequence? Viterbi
3 Learning What are A and B, given only observations? Forward–Backward (Baum-Welch, an EM algorithm)
Q4.4 Viterbi vs Forward — what is the one difference? [2 marks]
  • Identical recursion, identical table, identical emission multiply. Only the combination step differs:
    • Viterbi takes the MAX over previous states — and stores a backpointer, so it can reconstruct the best path.
    • Forward takes the SUM over previous states — the total probability of all paths. No backpointer.
  • MAX = "which single path is best". SUM = "how likely overall".
  • Learn one and you have both.
Q4.5 Why dynamic programming instead of enumerating all tag sequences? [1 mark]
  • With N states and T words there are N^T possible sequences — exponential, hopeless for a real sentence.
  • Viterbi reuses the best path into each state at each step, giving O(N²T). That reuse is the dynamic programming.

5 · Module 9 — Statistical Parsing

Q5.1 What is a PCFG and what problem does it solve? [2 marks]
  • A Probabilistic Context-Free Grammar: a CFG in which every rule carries a probability, and all rules expanding the same non-terminal sum to 1.
  • Problem solved — structural ambiguity. A plain CFG can tell you a sentence has five valid parses but not which one is meant. A PCFG ranks them: the probability of a parse is the product of the probabilities of every rule used, and the best parse is the highest product.
Q5.2 What is PP-attachment ambiguity? Give an example. [2 marks]
  • A prepositional phrase can attach to either the verb or the preceding noun, giving two different meanings.
  • "I saw the man with the telescope."
    • VP-attachment: with the telescope modifies saw → the telescope is the instrument I used.
    • NP-attachment: with the telescope modifies the man → the man was carrying it.
  • A PCFG chooses whichever attachment uses higher-probability rules.
Q5.3 Compare top-down and bottom-up parsing. [3 marks]
Top-down Bottom-up
Starts from the start symbol S the words
Direction expands rules until it reaches the words combines constituents until it reaches S
Wastes effort on trees that can never match the input words constituents that can never fit into an S
Guarantee every tree it builds is rooted in S every tree it builds is grounded in the input
Backtracking yes, on failure yes, on failure
Q5.4 What is CKY and what does it require of the grammar? [2 marks]
  • CKY is a bottom-up dynamic programming chart parser. It fills a triangular table where cell [i,j] holds every non-terminal that can span words i…j, building longer spans from shorter ones. Cost O(n³ × |G|).
  • Requirement: Chomsky Normal Form — every rule is either A → B C (exactly two non-terminals) or A → w (a single terminal).
  • Any CFG can be mechanically converted to CNF, so this costs no expressive power.
Q5.5 Name two weaknesses of a plain PCFG. [2 marks]
  1. No lexical sensitivity. Rule probabilities ignore the actual words, so the grammar cannot know that eat takes a food object. Fixed by lexicalisation — attaching head words to rules.
  2. Poor independence assumptions. Each expansion is assumed independent of context, but real expansions depend heavily on position — subject NPs are far more often pronouns than object NPs. Fixed by parent annotation / state splitting.

6 · Module 10 — Dependency Parsing

Q6.1 ⭐ Constituency vs dependency parsing — compare. [3 marks]
Constituency (phrase structure) Dependency
Represents how words group into nested phrases (NP, VP, PP) direct head → dependent relations between words
Nodes the words plus invented non-terminals only the words — nothing invented
Needs a CFG / PCFG a set of grammatical relations (nsubj, dobj, …)
Output a tree with internal phrase nodes labelled arcs over the sentence
Suits languages with fixed word order (English) languages with free word order (Hindi, Czech, Latin)
Downstream use grammar checking, phrase extraction relation extraction, QA, machine translation
Q6.2 ⭐ State the well-formedness conditions of a dependency tree. [3 marks]
  1. Single head — every word has exactly one incoming arc.
  2. Root — there is a single ROOT; nothing points into ROOT.
  3. Connected and acyclic — following arcs backwards from any word must reach ROOT. No cycles, no orphans.
  4. (optional) Projectivity — no crossing arcs when drawn above the sentence.

Use these as your checklist whenever you are asked to draw a tree. They catch essentially every mistake.

Q6.3 ⭐ Transition-based vs graph-based parsing. [3 marks]
Transition-based (arc-standard / arc-eager) Graph-based (MST)
How a stack + buffer; a classifier picks SHIFT / LEFT-ARC / RIGHT-ARC at each step score every possible arc, then find the maximum spanning tree
Search greedy, local — one pass, no going back global — all trees considered at once
Speed linear O(n) — very fast O(n²) or worse
Errors an early mistake propagates and can never be undone no error propagation
Long arcs weaker on long-distance dependencies better on long-distance dependencies
Non-projective not natively yes — Chu-Liu-Edmonds handles it
Q6.4 What is projectivity, and why does it matter? [2 marks]
  • A dependency tree is projective if, when every arc is drawn above the sentence in word order, no two arcs cross.
  • Equivalently: every word's descendants form a contiguous block.
  • Why it matters: the fast standard transition systems (arc-standard, arc-eager) can only produce projective trees. Freer-word-order languages produce non-projective structures, which need graph-based parsing or an extra SWAP transition.
Q6.5 Define head and dependent. How do you tell which is which? [2 marks]
  • The head determines the syntactic category and behaviour of the phrase; the dependent modifies or completes it.
  • Tests: the head can usually stand alone where the whole phrase stood; the dependent cannot. The head controls agreement.
  • "the red car" → head car, dependents the (det) and red (amod).
  • In "She ate rice", the verb ate is the head of the sentence and attaches to ROOT; She is nsubj, rice is dobj.
Q6.6 Why is Chu-Liu-Edmonds needed rather than "give each word its best head"? [2 marks]
  • Greedily giving every word its highest-scoring head satisfies "one head each", but it can create a cycle — A's best head is B and B's best head is A — and a cycle is not a tree.
  • Chu-Liu-Edmonds does exactly that greedy step, then detects each cycle, contracts it to a single node, and re-solves, repeating until nothing cycles. It is guaranteed to return the maximum-scoring valid tree.
  • If the greedy step happens to produce no cycle, you are already finished.

7 · Module 11 — Contextual Embeddings & Attention

Feb 2026 gave 3 of 6 marks here to pure theory. Mar 2026 gave 2.5 of 6. This section is the highest-yield reading in the file.

Q7.1 ⭐ What are contextual embeddings and why do they matter? [1.5 marks]
  • Vector representations of words whose values change with the surrounding context — the same word gets a different vector in every sentence.
  • Produced by transformer models such as BERT and ELMo.
  • Why they matter:
    • they capture word sense disambiguation — bank in a river sentence gets a different vector from bank in a finance sentence;
    • they encode better semantic and syntactic relationships;
    • they substantially improve downstream tasks (NER, QA, sentiment, tagging).
Q7.2 ⭐ How do contextual embeddings differ from static ones like Word2Vec? [1.5 marks]
Static (Word2Vec, GloVe) Contextual (BERT, ELMo)
Vectors per word one, fixed forever one per occurrence
Polysemy cannot handle it — all senses collapse into one vector handled — each sense gets its own vector
Computed from a global co-occurrence/prediction objective the actual surrounding sentence, via attention
Depends on position? no yes
Cost cheap, a lookup table expensive, a forward pass through the model
Q7.3 ⭐ Why do transformers use multiple attention heads instead of one? [1.5 marks]
  • Different words in a sentence relate to each other in many ways at once — a single averaging operation cannot capture all of them.
  • Multiple heads let the model:
    • attend to different representation subspaces;
    • capture different relationship types — syntactic, semantic, positional;
    • learn diverse patterns — one head tracks adjacent words, another subject–verb links, another coreference;
    • increase capacity without a proportional increase in computation, since the heads run in parallel and each is lower-dimensional.
Q7.4 Explain the roles of Query, Key and Value. [2 marks]

Think of a fuzzy lookup table.

  • Query (q) — the current focus of attention: the word asking "who here is relevant to me?"
  • Key (k) — each other word's advertisement: what it offers, used to be compared against the query.
  • Value (v) — each word's actual content, the thing that gets carried forward once its key matched.
  • Keys and values always come in pairs. You match on the key but you propagate the value.
  • All three come from the same input in self-attention: q = Qx, k = Kx, v = Vx.
Q7.5 ⭐ Why divide by √d_k in scaled dot-product attention? [1.5 marks]
  • Dot products grow with dimension: with d_k terms in the sum, the variance of q·k scales with d_k, so scores become large in magnitude.
  • Large scores push softmax into a region where one weight is ≈1 and the rest ≈0 — it saturates, and its gradients vanish, so the model trains badly.
  • Dividing by √d_k keeps the scores at a sensible scale. The handout describes it as acting as regularisation that improves performance of larger models.
  • Formula: SelfAttention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √d_k) V
Q7.6 Self-attention has no notion of word order. How is that fixed? [1.5 marks]
  • Self-attention is a weighted average — permuting the input words permutes the output identically. It is order-blind; "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" would produce the same set of representations.
  • Fix: positional encodings — a position-dependent vector is added to each input embedding before the first layer, so position information is carried in the vectors themselves.
  • Either fixed sinusoidal functions or learned position embeddings.
Q7.7 List the barriers to using self-attention as a building block, and the fix for each. [3 marks]
Barrier Fix
No inherent notion of order add position representations to the inputs
No non-linearity — it is all just weighted averages apply the same feed-forward network to each self-attention output
Must not look at the future when predicting a sequence mask future positions by setting those attention weights to 0 (masked self-attention, used in language modelling and MT decoding)
Q7.8 Self-attention vs cross-attention vs masked self-attention. [2 marks]
  • Self-attention — Q, K and V all come from the same sequence. Every position may attend to every other.
  • Masked (causal) self-attention — same, but positions after the current one are masked out, so the model cannot cheat by seeing the future. Used in decoders and language models.
  • Cross-attention — the queries come from the decoder, while the keys and values come from the encoder. This is how the decoder reads the source sentence in translation.
Q7.9 How does multi-head attention combine its heads? [1 mark]
  • Each head has its own W^Q, W^K, W^V matrices and produces its own output vector.
  • The head outputs are concatenated and then projected back down to dimension d by a matrix W^O, so the output is the same size as the input and layers can be stacked.
  • MultiHeadAttention(X) = (head₁ ⊕ head₂ ⊕ … ⊕ head_h) W^O
  • Exam check: the number of rows of W^O must equal the length of the concatenated vector.
Q7.10 What problem did attention solve in the original encoder-decoder? [2 marks]
  • The bottleneck problem. Without attention, the decoder sees a single fixed context vector — the encoder's last hidden state — which must contain the entire source sentence. Long sentences do not fit.
  • With attention, the decoder gets a different, dynamic context vector at each step, computed from all the encoder hidden states.
  • Extra benefits stated in the slides: it helps with vanishing gradients by giving a shortcut to faraway states, and it gives interpretability — the attention distribution shows what the decoder was looking at.
Q7.11 Given two heads with weights [0.1, 0.2, 0.7] and [0.5, 0.3, 0.2] over ("the", "cat", "sat") — what is each head specialising in? [1 mark]

This is the Mar 2026 Q4(d) question. The method: find the largest weight, name the word, then say what kind of relation that word represents.

  • Head 1 puts 0.7 on "sat" — the verb. It appears to track the action / predicate, i.e. a syntactic subject–verb relationship.
  • Head 2 puts 0.5 on "the" — a determiner. It appears to track grammatical / structural information rather than content.
  • Together they demonstrate that different heads specialise in different linguistic patterns, which is exactly why multiple heads are used.

8 · Module 12 — Word Sense & WordNet

Q8.1 ⭐ Define homonymy, polysemy, synonymy, antonymy — with examples. [3 marks]
Term Meaning Example
Homonymy same form, unrelated meanings bank (river edge) vs bank (financial)
Polysemy same form, related meanings bank (the institution) vs bank (the building it occupies)
Synonymy different forms, (nearly) the same meaning big / large, couch / sofa
Antonymy opposite meaning along one dimension hot / cold, rise / fall

Sub-types of homonymy: homophones (same sound — write/right), homographs (same spelling — bass the fish / bass the sound).

Q8.2 Define hyponymy, hypernymy and meronymy. [2 marks]
  • Hyponym — a more specific term. dog is a hyponym of animal.
  • Hypernym — the more general term. animal is a hypernym of dog. (Mnemonic: hyper = above = broader.)
  • Meronym — a part of something. wheel is a meronym of car. The reverse, car, is the holonym.
Q8.3 What is WordNet? Describe its structure. [2 marks]
  • A large lexical database of English, organised by meaning rather than by spelling.
  • The unit is the synset — a set of synonyms that share one sense, together with a gloss (a short definition) and usually usage examples.
  • Synsets are linked by semantic relations: hypernym/hyponym (the "is-a" hierarchy), meronym/holonym, antonym.
  • Separate hierarchies for nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs.
  • Used for WSD, semantic similarity, query expansion and as a sense inventory.
Q8.4 What is Word Sense Disambiguation and why is it hard? [2 marks]
  • Deciding which sense of an ambiguous word is intended in a given context — choosing one synset from the inventory.
  • Hard because: senses are fine-grained and overlapping; the useful context may be far away in the text or absent entirely; and hand-labelled sense data is expensive, so there is a knowledge-acquisition bottleneck.
  • Matters for machine translation (different senses translate differently), information retrieval, and text-to-speech (bass the fish vs the sound).
Q8.5 Explain the Lesk algorithm. What is its main weakness? [2 marks]
  • Idea: the correct sense is the one whose dictionary gloss shares the most words with the context sentence.
  • Simplified Lesk: for each candidate sense, count the overlap between the gloss (plus usage examples) and the content words of the sentence; pick the highest count. Ties are broken by the most frequent sense.
  • Weakness: glosses are very short, so overlaps are tiny and often zero — the method is brittle and sensitive to exact wording. Extended Lesk adds the glosses of related synsets to enlarge the signature.
Q8.6 ⭐ Why does WordNet perform poorly on domain-specific text, and what is the fix? [2 marks]

This is the Mar 2026 Q5A question, almost verbatim.

  • WordNet is a general-purpose lexical database. Specialised medical, legal and technical vocabulary is missing or only thinly defined.
  • Domain terms also carry domain-specific senses that the general inventory does not list, so the correct sense may simply not be an option.
  • Fix: combine WordNet with a domain-specific ontology or lexicon — for example UMLS/SNOMED in medicine — or extend it with terms mined from a domain corpus. This adds expert knowledge and improves accuracy.
Q8.7 Name the three families of WSD approaches. [2 marks]
  1. Knowledge-based — use a dictionary or WordNet, no labelled data. Example: Lesk, and graph-based measures over the WordNet hierarchy.
  2. Supervised — train a classifier on hand-labelled sense examples using context features. Most accurate, but limited by labelled-data scarcity.
  3. Semi-supervised / unsupervised — bootstrapping from a few seeds (Yarowsky), or clustering contexts into senses (word sense induction).

9 · Module 13 — Semantic Web & Ontology

The scheme says Application / Case study — there is no numerical here. Every mark in this module is prose or triples. Do not skip it because it has no formula.

Q9.1 What is the Semantic Web, in one idea? [2 marks]
  • A vision of the Web as a huge queryable database rather than a pile of documents: data published so that machines can understand and combine it, not just display it.
  • Achieved by publishing data as RDF triples with globally unique URIs, so that a resource named in one dataset is recognisably the same resource in another.
  • That shared naming is what lets independent datasets merge automatically — the basis of Linked Open Data and projects like DBpedia.
Q9.2 ⭐ What is an RDF triple? Give the data model. [2 marks]
  • RDF represents every fact as a subject – predicate – object triple.
    • Subject — the entity being described (a URI)
    • Predicate — the property or relation (a URI)
    • Object — the value: either another resource (a URI) or a literal
  • A set of triples forms a directed labelled graph: subjects and objects are nodes, predicates are the edges.
  • Example: (Anita, enrolledIn, DataScience).
Q9.3 ⭐ RDF vs RDFS vs OWL — which does what? [2 marks]
RDF RDFS OWL
Purpose represent data as triples add a simple vocabulary/schema add logic and reasoning
Gives you subject–predicate–object facts classes, subclasses, properties, domain and range restrictions, cardinality, disjointness, equivalence, inverse and transitive properties
Can it infer? no limited (subclass inheritance) yes — a reasoner derives new facts and detects inconsistency
Use it when you are storing facts you need a light taxonomy you must enforce constraints or infer

The exam phrasing: storing book data → RDF. Checking "every book must have an author" → OWL, because that is a constraint requiring reasoning.

Q9.4 ⭐ Case study: model papers, authors and conferences using RDF and OWL. [2 marks]

This is the Mar 2026 Q5C answer pattern. Always answer in two halves — RDF for the facts, OWL for the rules.

With RDF — represent the factual data. Entities become resources linked by triples:

(Paper1, hasAuthor,   Author1)
(Paper1, presentedAt, Conference1)
(Author1, affiliatedWith, University1)

With OWL — define the semantics and constraints.

  • Classes: Paper, Author, Conference
  • Object properties: hasAuthor (domain Paper, range Author), presentedAt (domain Paper, range Conference)
  • Constraints: every Paper must have at least one hasAuthor (minimum cardinality 1); Paper and Author are disjoint classes; hasAuthor has the inverse property authorOf.
  • A reasoner can then flag a paper with no author as inconsistent, and infer authorOf links automatically.
Q9.5 Case study: identify subjects, predicates and objects for a university system. [2 marks]

The Feb 2026 Q6 pattern. Answer in three labelled lists, then the triples.

  • Entities (subjects): Student (Anita), Course (Data Science), Faculty (Dr. Rao), Department (Computer Science)
  • Properties (predicates): enrolledIn, taughtBy, memberOf, hasName, offeredBy
  • Values (objects): the course resource, the faculty resource, the department resource, and literals such as the name string

Triples:

(Anita,        enrolledIn, DataScience)
(DataScience,  taughtBy,   Dr_Rao)
(Dr_Rao,       memberOf,   ComputerScienceDept)
Q9.6 Why are URIs essential to the Semantic Web? [1 mark]
  • A URI is a globally unique name for a resource. Two datasets that both use http://dbpedia.org/resource/India are demonstrably talking about the same thing, so their graphs can be merged without manual reconciliation.
  • Plain strings cannot do this — "India" in one database and "India" in another might be a country, a film or a person.
Q9.7 Name three application areas of ontologies. [1.5 marks]
  • Information retrieval and search — query expansion using the class hierarchy; semantic rather than keyword matching.
  • Data integration — merging heterogeneous databases under a shared vocabulary (the Linked Open Data cloud, DBpedia).
  • Knowledge-based systems and reasoning — biomedical ontologies, e-commerce product taxonomies, enterprise knowledge graphs powering Graph RAG.

10 · Module 14 — Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Q10.1 ⭐ What is RAG and what problem does it solve? [2 marks]
  • Retrieval-Augmented Generation: before answering, the system retrieves relevant documents from an external store and puts them into the LLM's prompt, so the model answers grounded in those documents.
  • Problems it solves:
    • Hallucination — the model invents plausible but false facts. RAG grounds it in retrieved evidence.
    • Stale knowledge — the model's training has a cutoff. Retrieval supplies up-to-date information without retraining.
    • No access to private data — company documents were never in training.
    • No citations — RAG can point at the source it used.
Q10.2 Describe the RAG pipeline end to end. [3 marks]
  1. Ingestion — documents are loaded and chunked into passages.
  2. Embedding — each chunk is converted to a vector and stored in a vector database.
  3. Query embedding — the user's question is embedded with the same model.
  4. Retrieval — nearest-neighbour / similarity search returns the top-k chunks, usually filtered by a similarity threshold.
  5. Augmentation — the retrieved chunks are inserted into the prompt alongside the question.
  6. Generation — the LLM answers using that context, ideally citing it.
Q10.3 ⭐ Why is chunk overlap used? [2 marks]
  • Chunk boundaries are arbitrary. A sentence or an idea that straddles a boundary gets split in half, and neither half retrieves well or reads coherently.
  • Overlap repeats the last few tokens of one chunk at the start of the next, so context is preserved across the boundary and no idea is orphaned.
  • Cost: overlap duplicates content, increasing storage and the risk of near identical chunks being retrieved together, wasting context window.
  • Typical: 10–20% of the chunk size.
Q10.4 ⭐ What competes for space in the LLM context window? [2 marks]
  • Every LLM has a fixed context window measured in tokens. Everything must fit inside it:
    • the system prompt / instructions
    • the user query
    • the retrieved chunks
    • the conversation history
    • and the space reserved for the generated answer
  • This is why retrieval is thresholded and top-k limited: adding more chunks is not free, and eventually crowds out the answer itself.
Q10.5 ⭐ Traditional (vector) RAG vs Graph RAG. [3 marks]
Traditional / Vector RAG Graph RAG
Store vector database of text chunks knowledge graph of entities and relations
Retrieval semantic similarity to the query traversal of typed relationships
Strong on "find me passages about X" multi-hop questions — "who supervised the author of X?"
Weak on questions needing several linked facts; it returns similar text, not connected facts fuzzy, unstructured, semantic queries
Explainability returns a passage returns an explicit path through the graph
Build cost low — chunk and embed high — entity/relation extraction and curation

Many systems use both: the graph for structure, the vector store for free text.

Q10.6 What is Agentic RAG? [2 marks]
  • A framework in which the LLM acts as an agent that decides how to retrieve, rather than a passive generator handed a fixed set of chunks.
  • Components: an LLM agent orchestrating reasoning; a set of tools (vector search, graph query, web search, calculators); memory; and a planning loop.
  • The agent can reformulate the query, retrieve repeatedly, judge whether the evidence is sufficient, and only then answer.
  • Better on complex multi-step questions; costs more calls and more latency.
Q10.7 What is Multimodal RAG? [1 mark]
  • Ingestion handles more than text: documents such as PDFs are broken down with text extracted, images isolated, tables parsed, and each modality is embedded (often into a shared space).
  • Retrieval can then return an image or a table as evidence, and a multimodal model reasons over text and images together.
Q10.8 How is retrieval quality measured? Define precision and recall here. [2 marks]
  • Precision = relevant chunks retrieved ÷ total chunks retrieved. "Of what I showed the model, how much was useful?" Low precision means contextual noise, which degrades generation quality and wastes the window.
  • Recall = relevant chunks retrieved ÷ total relevant chunks that exist. "Of what was out there, how much did I find?" Low recall means the answer may be missing entirely.
  • Trade-off: lowering the similarity threshold raises recall and lowers precision. F1 = 2PR / (P + R) balances them.

Q11.2 ⭐ Classify the types of summarization by input and by user need. [3 marks]

By number of documents:

  • Single-document — one document in, one summary out. Produces a headline, abstract or outline.
  • Multi-document — a set of documents on the same event. Must remove redundancy and resolve contradictions across sources. Used for news aggregation.

By user need:

  • Generic — summarises the overall content, with no particular query. It ignores user intent by design.
  • Query-focused (topic-based) — tailored to a specific information need; effectively a form of complex question answering. It extracts only the parts relevant to the query and ignores the rest.
Q11.3 ⭐ Scenario drill — name the summarization type for each. [3 marks]

This is the exact Mar 2026 Q7 format. Read the scenario, and ask two questions: how many documents? and is there a query?

Scenario Type
A journalist uploads one long report and wants a headline and a short abstract Single-document, generic
An analyst gives 15 articles about the same summit and wants one overview Multi-document
A researcher asks "summarise the sections about economic impact" Query-focused

And the follow-up you will be asked: why won't one approach do for all three? — because the scenarios differ in input structure (one vs many documents), user intent (no query vs an explicit query), and output requirement (headline vs gist vs targeted answer). Generic summarization ignores intent; query-focused summarization requires a query to exist; multi-document summarization must additionally manage redundancy and contradiction across sources.

Q11.4 ⭐ What is MMR and what problem does it solve? [2 marks]
  • Maximal Marginal Relevance. A greedy sentence-selection criterion that balances relevance to the query against redundancy with what has already been selected.
  • MMR(s) = λ · sim(s, q) − (1 − λ) · sim(s, S) where q is the query/topic and S is the set already chosen.
  • Problem solved: picking sentences purely by relevance selects the same fact restated several times. The second term penalises a sentence for resembling the summary so far.
  • λ = 1 → pure relevance, maximum redundancy. λ = 0 → pure novelty, possibly off-topic. In between, a diverse and relevant summary.
Q11.5 How do you justify an MMR selection in words? [2 marks]

Marks are given for the reasoning, not the arithmetic. The template:

Although S2 is more relevant to the topic (higher sim(s,q)), it is highly similar to the sentence already selected, so it is redundant and its sim(s,S) penalty is large. S1 contributes new, complementary information and therefore scores higher under MMR. MMR deliberately trades a little relevance for coverage and diversity, which is what makes a summary informative rather than repetitive.

Always name both halves — relevance and redundancy — and say which one decided the outcome.

Q11.6 What is ROUGE and what are its limitations? [2 marks]
  • ROUGE — Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation. It measures n-gram overlap between a generated summary and one or more human reference summaries.
  • ROUGE-N — overlap of n-grams (ROUGE-1 unigrams, ROUGE-2 bigrams). ROUGE-L — longest common subsequence, which rewards word order.
  • Mainly recall-oriented: how much of the reference the summary covers.
  • Limitations: it rewards surface overlap, so a correct paraphrase scores badly and a fluent nonsense summary made of reference words scores well. It ignores factuality, coherence and readability, and depends heavily on which reference summaries you happen to have.
Q11.7 Name three applications of summarization. [1.5 marks]
  • News aggregation — one overview from many outlets covering an event.
  • Headline and snippet generation — for publishing and search results.
  • Meeting, email and report digests; scientific abstract generation; legal and medical document review, where the volume makes full reading impossible.
Q11.8 What are the standard stages of an extractive summarizer? [2 marks]
  1. Content selection — score every sentence for importance (position, cue phrases, topic-word overlap, centrality) and choose the best ones.
  2. Information ordering — arrange the selected sentences into a coherent sequence, usually by original document order for a single document.
  3. Sentence realisation — clean up: compress the sentences, fix dangling pronouns and broken references so the summary reads as text rather than a list.

MMR sits inside stage 1, adding the anti-redundancy term to the score.
