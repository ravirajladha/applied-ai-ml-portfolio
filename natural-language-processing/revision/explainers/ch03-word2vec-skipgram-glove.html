1 · The trick: train a classifier, then throw it away

Analogy. You want to know which staff in a big office are on the same team. You can't ask. So you set up a silly game: "guess whether these two people were seen at lunch together." You play it thousands of times, and to get good at it your brain builds a mental map of who hangs around whom. Then you delete the game and keep the map. The map is the embeddings.

We don't actually care about the task. We only want the weights it was forced to learn.

Self-supervision. Nobody labels anything. A word c that genuinely occurs near apricot in the corpus acts as the gold "correct answer". The text labels itself — which is why word2vec can train on billions of words for free.

Memory hook. Skip-gram skips outward from the centre to the context. CBOW collects the context coming inward to the centre. Arrow out vs arrow in.

3 · Build the training data (positives + negatives)

The number to remember. Mikolov et al. 2013: k = 2–5 for large datasets, k = 5–20 for small datasets. Small corpus → you need more negative contrast per positive to get a usable signal.

4 · ⭐ One SGNS training step, live

These are the exact numbers from the slide's "Ned Stark" worked example. Change the learning rate and watch every downstream number move.

corpus : "Ned Stark is the most honourable man" positive pair : (ned, stark) t = 1 negative words : pimples, zebra, idiot t = 0 input embedding w(ned) = [-0.018, 0.404, -0.317]
learning rate η 0.05
The sign is the whole story. The positive pair gets a negative error → the update pulls the vectors together. Every negative sample gets a positive error → the update pushes them apart. "Move apricot toward jam, away from Tolstoy." That is literally all gradient descent is doing here.
Exam favourite. SGNS learns TWO matrices — the target matrix W and the context matrix C, each holding one embedding per vocabulary word. It is common to just add them: word i is represented as wᵢ + cᵢ. Saying "word2vec learns one matrix" loses the mark.

5 · Window size changes what "similar" means

Why this happens. A tight window only sees a word's immediate grammatical neighbours, so it learns what kind of thing it is. A wide window sees the whole topic of the sentence, so it learns what it's associated with. Small = similarity. Large = relatedness. Straight back to the coffee/tea vs coffee/cup distinction from Chapter 2.

6 · Analogies — the parallelogram

For a : a* :: b : b*, compute a* − a + b and take the nearest word.

What the picture shows. The vector from man to king and the vector from woman to queen are the same arrow — that arrow is the "royalty" relation, encoded as a direction in space. Add it to any word and you move along that relation. The method works with both sparse and dense embeddings (Turney & Littman 2005; Mikolov et al. 2013b).

7 · GloVe — count vs predict vs both

TrainingFastSlower, scales with corpus sizeFast
StatisticsEfficiently usedInefficiently usedEfficiently used
GloVe = Global Vectors for word representation. Stanford (Pennington, Socher, Manning).

Key idea: learning should be based on ratios of co-occurrence probabilities, rather than the probabilities themselves.

THE ONE-LINE EXAM ANSWER The advantage of GloVe is that, unlike word2vec, it does not rely only on LOCAL statistics (local context information of words) but incorporates GLOBAL statistics (word-word co-occurrence) to obtain word vectors.
Do not confuse two different axes.
Axis 1 — sparse vs dense: counting vs predicting. word2vec and GloVe are both dense.
Axis 2 — static vs contextual: word2vec and GloVe are both static (one vector per word type, so bank the river and bank the institution share it). ELMo and BERT are contextual (one vector per token, computed from its actual context).

8 · Embeddings carry bias

Ask the parallelogram model for analogies and you get:

AnalogyAnswer
Paris : France :: Tokyo : xJapan✅ fine
father : doctor :: mother : xnurse⚠️ bias
man : computer programmer :: woman : xhomemaker⚠️ bias

Bolukbasi et al., NeurIPS 2016 — "Man is to computer programmer as woman is to homemaker? Debiasing word embeddings".

Why it matters, in one line. Algorithms that use embeddings inside, say, a hiring search for programmers might therefore lead to bias in hiring. The embedding didn't invent the bias — it faithfully learned it from the corpus, which is exactly the problem.
Embeddings as a historical instrument. Train on different decades (~30 million books, 1850–1990, Google Books) and you can watch meanings shift (Hamilton, Leskovec & Jurafsky, ACL 2016). Garg et al. (PNAS 2018) scored adjectives for gender/ethnic bias per decade:
  • Competence adjectives (smart, wise, brilliant, resourceful, thoughtful, logical) were biased toward men — a bias slowly decreasing 1960–1990.
  • Dehumanizing adjectives (barbaric, monstrous, bizarre) were biased toward Asians in the 1930s, decreasing across the 20th century.
These match the results of actual surveys done in the 1930s — which is what makes the method credible rather than a curiosity.
