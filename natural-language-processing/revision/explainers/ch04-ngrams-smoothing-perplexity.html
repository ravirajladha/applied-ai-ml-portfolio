1 · What a language model actually does

Analogy. Your phone keyboard suggesting the next word. That's it. That's a language model. It has seen a lot of text, and it knows that after "I'll be there in five" the word minutes is far likelier than elephants. Everything in this chapter is that idea plus arithmetic.

A model that computes P(W) — the probability of a sentence — or P(wₙ | w₁ … wₙ₋₁) — the probability of an upcoming word — is a language model.

ApplicationWhat the LM decides
Machine translationP(high winds tonight) > P(large winds tonight)
Spell correctionP(about fifteen minutes from) > P(about fifteen minuets from)
Speech recognitionP(I saw a van) >> P(eyes awe of an)

Look hard at the third one — those two candidates sound almost identical out loud. Only the language model can tell them apart.

2 · Why the chain rule alone fails

The chain rule is exactly correct:

P(its water is so transparent) = P(its) × P(water | its) × P(is | its water) × P(so | its water is) × P(transparent | its water is so)

…and every factor is estimated by counting:

P(the | its water is so transparent) = count(its water is so transparent the) ----------------------------------------- count(its water is so transparent)
Why it cannot work. You would never see most of those long strings even once in any corpus, so almost every numerator is 0. Too many parameters, far too little data. Fix: the Markov assumption — limit the history to a fixed number of words.

Do not forget <s> and </s>. The start symbol gives the first word a context; the end symbol makes the probabilities of all sentences of all lengths sum to 1. Dropping </s> is the single most common lost mark in this chapter.
Why n-grams are, in general, insufficient. The slide's example:
"The computer(s) which I had just put into the machine room on the fifth floor is (are) crashing."
The agreement is decided by a word nine positions back. No practical n-gram sees that far. (This is precisely the gap neural LMs and then Transformers close.)

4 · The zero problem — watch it kill everything

Analogy. A judge scores a gymnast by multiplying her marks for six apparatus. She's brilliant on five and the sixth judge simply never saw her perform, so writes 0. Final score: zero. Not "slightly lower" — zero. That is what one unseen bigram does to a whole test set.
Training set Test set … denied the allegations … denied the offer … denied the reports … denied the loan … denied the claims … denied the request P("offer" | denied the) = 0
The real reason smoothing is non-optional. It isn't that the model becomes inaccurate. It's that perplexity is 1/P, so P = 0 makes perplexity infinite — you cannot even evaluate the model. It stops being bad and starts being undefined.

5 · ⭐ Add-1 smoothing, and the damage it does

Analogy. A new restaurant has 3 reviews, all 5 stars. Is it really the best restaurant in the city? Add-1 smoothing is the site quietly adding one imaginary average review to everybody, so three real 5-stars can't beat three hundred. It protects you from zeros, but it also punishes genuinely strong evidence — and that is exactly the complaint against it.

RECONSTITUTED COUNT — what the smoothed count "would have been" c*(i,want) = (827 + 1) x 2533 / (2533 + 1446) = 828 x 2533 / 3979 = 2,097,324 / 3979 = 527 (down from 827!)
Two traps in one formula.
1. The denominator adds V, not 1. And V is the number of unique words (types) — not the corpus size.
2. P(to|want) fell from 0.66 to 0.26. Add-1 took a well-attested bigram and cut it by 60%, because it had to hand probability mass to 1446 words that never followed want.
The conclusion the slide draws. Add-1 is NOT used for n-grams — better methods exist (Good-Turing, Kneser-Ney). But add-1 is used to smooth other NLP models: text classification, and any domain where the number of zeros isn't so huge.

6 · Backoff vs interpolation

Analogy. You're asked to guess someone's salary. Backoff: if you know their exact job title and city, use that; if not, fall back to just their job title; if not, fall back to the national average — one source at a time. Interpolation: always blend all three, weighted. Interpolation works better, because even a good trigram estimate benefits from a little smoothing by its shorter cousins.

7 · ⭐ Perplexity — the branching factor

Analogy — the only intuition you need. Perplexity is "how many equally likely options does the model feel like it's choosing between at each word?" A model with perplexity 10 is as confused as someone guessing a random digit 0–9. A model with perplexity 2 is basically flipping a coin. Lower = more confident = better.

The Shannon game

I always order pizza with cheese and ____ mushrooms 0.1 pepperoni 0.1 anchovies 0.01 ... fried rice 0.0001 ... and 1e-100 The 33rd President of the US was ____ I saw a ____

Unigrams are terrible at this game. Why? They ignore the context completely and always predict the same distribution — "cheese and" tells them nothing.

8 · Unknown words and <UNK>

Closed vocabulary taskWe know all the words in advance; V is fixed
Open vocabulary taskWe don't. Unseen words are OOV — Out Of Vocabulary
THE <UNK> RECIPE 1. Create a fixed lexicon L of size V. 2. At the TEXT NORMALISATION phase, change any training word NOT in L to <UNK>. 3. Now TRAIN <UNK>'s probabilities LIKE A NORMAL WORD. 4. At decoding time, use the <UNK> probabilities for any unseen input word.

Step 3 is the clever bit — you deliberately create training examples of "a word I've never seen" so the model has a real estimate for it.

Also worth a mark: work in log space. log(p₁ × p₂ × p₃) = log p₁ + log p₂ + log p₃. Two reasons: avoid underflow (multiplying thousands of small probabilities drives the product to 0 in floating point), and adding is faster than multiplying.
