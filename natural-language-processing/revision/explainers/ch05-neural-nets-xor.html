3 · ⭐ The XOR problem — see why a line fails

Analogy. You're sorting a pile of coins into two trays with a single straight ruler laid on the table. AND and OR can be split by a ruler. XOR cannot — the two "yes" coins sit on opposite diagonal corners, and no straight line separates diagonals from diagonals. You need a second step.

A perceptron = a very simple unit: binary output (0 or 1), and no non-linear activation function. Its equation w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b = 0 is the equation of a line — a decision boundary.

Try the line:
Minsky and Papert, 1969. Showing that a perceptron cannot compute XOR is the result that caused the first AI winter. XOR is not a linearly separable function — and that sentence is the answer to the exam question.

The point of the hidden layer. Watch the right-hand plot: the hidden layer maps [0,1] and [1,0] onto the SAME point [1,0]. In h-space the problem has become linearly separable, so a single output weight finishes the job. With learning, hidden layers learn to form useful representations — that sentence is the whole justification for deep learning, and it is worth quoting verbatim.
Counting convention. We do not count the input layer. A "2-layer network" has one hidden layer and one output layer. Also: a feedforward net is also called a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) for historical reasons — a misnomer, since its units are not perceptrons.

5 · Softmax calculator

Analogy. Sigmoid answers "yes or no?". Softmax answers "which of these k things?" — it takes any list of scores and turns it into a set of percentages that add up to 100%. Exponentiating first is what makes the biggest score dominate.

Sigmoid vs softmax — the one-liner. Sigmoid for 2 classes (one output unit, a scalar). Softmax for more than 2 (one output unit per class, a vector that sums to 1). Softmax is the generalisation of sigmoid.

7 · ⭐ Why neural LMs beat n-grams (cat / dog)

Analogy. A trigram model is a colleague with a perfect memory and zero imagination — if it never heard the exact phrase, it has nothing. A neural LM is a colleague who understands that dogs and cats are both pets, so anything learned about one transfers to the other. Same evidence, wildly different generalisation.
TRAINING DATA "I have to make sure that the CAT gets fed." NEVER SEEN "dog gets fed" TEST DATA "I forgot to make sure that the DOG gets ____"

Arbitrary length problem → sliding windows of fixed length. A one-hot vector times E just selects one row, so in practice you use a specialised lookup function rather than a matrix multiply.

The honest conclusion on the slide. Neural LMs handle longer histories, generalise over similar words and predict more accurately — but they are more complex, slower, need more energy to train, and are less interpretable. For many (especially smaller) tasks, an n-gram language model is still the right tool.

Why backpropagation exists — the summary sentence. For training we need the derivative of the loss with respect to weights in early layers, but the loss is computed only at the very end of the network. Backward differentiation solves this: given a computation graph and the derivatives of all the functions in it, we can automatically compute the derivative of the loss with respect to those early weights.
