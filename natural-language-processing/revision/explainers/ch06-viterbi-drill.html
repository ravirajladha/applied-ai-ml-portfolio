1 · Why tagging isn't dictionary lookup

Analogy. The word "lead" on its own is unanswerable — metal or verb? You need the sentence. Tagging is not "look the word up", it's "decide, using the neighbours". And because every neighbour is also ambiguous, you can't decide them one at a time — you have to decide the whole sequence at once. That's the whole reason Viterbi exists.

POS tagging = assigning a part-of-speech or lexical class marker to each word in a sentence.

Input : the lead paint is unsafe Output: the/Det lead/N paint/N is/V unsafe/Adj

One word, four tags — click to see it move

Why you cannot classify each word independently — the two reasons from the slide:
  1. It is not easy to integrate information from the categories of tokens on both sides.
  2. It is difficult to propagate uncertainty between decisions and collectively determine the most likely joint assignment for the whole sequence.
Hence: probabilistic sequence models. The standard one is the Hidden Markov Model.

2 · Word classes and the Penn tagset

Closed class

A small fixed membership. Usually function words — short common words that play a role in grammar.

Prepositions : of, in, by, ... Auxiliaries : may, can, will, had, been, ... Pronouns : I, you, she, mine, his, them, ...

Open class

New ones can be created all the time.

English has exactly FOUR: Nouns Verbs Adjectives Adverbs Many languages have these four — but not all.

Penn Treebank — 45 tags. Hover-worthy subset:

DT DeterminerVB Verb, baseNN Noun, sing.
NNS Noun, pluralVBD Verb, pastNNP Proper noun
JJ AdjectiveVBG Verb, gerundRB Adverb
VBN Verb, past part.VBZ Verb, 3sg presMD Modal
IN Prep / sub. conj.TO the word toPRP Pers. pronoun
The/DT grand/JJ jury/NN commented/VBD on/IN a/DT number/NN of/IN other/JJ topics/NNS ./.
Two conventions worth a mark. Prepositions and subordinating conjunctions are both IN ("although/IN I/PRP…") — except the preposition to, which gets its own tag TO.

3 · The two probabilities (and the trap)

NameFormulaIntuitionCounted as
Transition
tag → tag		 P(tᵢ | tᵢ₋₁) Determiners precede adjectives and nouns — That/DT flight/NN, The/DT yellow/JJ hat/NN — so P(NN|DT) and P(JJ|DT) are high C(tᵢ₋₁,tᵢ) / C(tᵢ₋₁)
Emission
tag → WORD		 P(wᵢ | tᵢ) VBZ (3sg present verb) is likely to be is, so P(is|VBZ) is high C(tᵢ,wᵢ) / C(tᵢ)
THE trap of this chapter. Emission is P(word | tag) — not P(tag | word). Writing it upside down costs the whole question, because it silently turns your generative HMM into a discriminative model.

Mnemonic: the hidden state emits the word. Tag first, word second.

The derivation, in four lines

We want: t^ = argmax P(T | W) Bayes rule: P(W|T) P(T) t^ = argmax ------------- P(W) P(W) is the SAME for every candidate tag sequence, so DROP it: t^ = argmax P(W|T) · P(T) \______/ \____/ LIKELIHOOD PRIOR Two assumptions: (1) MARKOV P(T) ~ PROD P(ti | ti-1) (2) INDEPENDENCE P(W|T) ~ PROD P(wi | ti) THE HMM TAGGING EQUATION t^ = argmax PROD P(wi | ti) · P(ti | ti-1) \_________/ \___________/ emission transition

4 · Markov chains — the off-by-one that costs marks

A set of STATES Q = q1, q2 ... qN TRANSITION probabilities A = {aij}, aij = P(qt = j | qt-1 = i) INITIAL probability vector pi MARKOV PROPERTY: the current state depends ONLY on the previous state.
π (initial) a (self-loop) days
The off-by-one. T states means T − 1 transitions. Four warm days is π₃ · a₃₃³ — three multiplications of a, not four. Push "days" up and down above and watch the exponent. This is a classic dropped mark.

5 · ⭐⭐ THE VITERBI DRILL

Analogy. You're planning a road trip across 4 cities, and each city has 5 hotels. There are 5⁴ = 625 possible itineraries. But you don't need to price them all: once you know the cheapest way to reach each hotel in city 2, you can throw away every worse route into it forever. Viterbi is exactly that — keep one best path into each state, at each step.
INITIALISATION V[t,1] = P(t | <S>) · P(w1 | t) RECURSION V[t,j] = max [ V[t', j-1] · P(t | t') ] · P(wj | t) t' \___________________________/ \__________/ best way to ARRIVE in state t emission bp[t,j] = argmax [ V[t',j-1] · P(t|t') ] TERMINATION best = max V[t,T] · P(STOP | t) BACKTRACE follow the backpointers back to column 1 COMPLEXITY O(N² T)
sentence
Press ▶ Next cell to start.
The single most important habit. The max is taken over where you came from, and the emission P(wⱼ|t) multiplies in once, AFTER the max. Do not put the emission inside the max. Watch the working panel above — it always shows the candidates first, then the max, then the emission.
Note on the STOP column. The slide's worked example reads the answer straight off the last column. If your exam question supplies an explicit STOP / </s> transition, do the termination step properly — multiply each final cell by P(STOP|t) before taking the max.

6 · The "race" disambiguation, calculated

Secretariat/NNP is/VBZ expected/VBN to/TO race/?? tomorrow/NR NR = adverbial noun ("tomorrow") TO = the word "to"
Notice WHICH factor decided it. The emission probabilities actually favour NN (0.00057 > 0.00012) — race really is more often a noun. But P(VB|TO) = .83 vs P(NN|TO) = .00047 is a ~1770× advantage that steamrolls it. The transition probability overrules the word's own preference. That is the sequence model earning its keep, in one example.

7 · Ice cream — the other classic setup

The setup. You are a climatologist in 2799 studying global warming. You cannot find any weather records for Baltimore in summer 2007 — but you find Jason Eisner's diary listing how many ice creams he ate each day. Your job: figure out how hot it was each day.
Observations (VISIBLE) : 1, 2, 3, 2, 2, 2, 3 ... ice creams eaten Hidden states : H, C, H, H, H, C, C ... Hot / Cold

For the observation 1 3 1 with 2 states there are 2³ = 8 possible state sequences: HHH, HHC, HCH, HCC, CCC, CCH, CHC, CHH. Score one of them by hand:

path C H C, observation 1 3 1 P(Cold|Start) 0.2 x P(1 | Cold) 0.5 x P(Hot | Cold) 0.4 x P(3 | Hot) 0.4 x P(Cold | Hot) 0.3 x P(1 | Cold) 0.5 ------------------------ = 0.0024

Viterbi is what saves you from doing that eight times — or, for a 20-word sentence with 45 tags, 45²⁰ ≈ 10³³ times.

8 · HMM vs MEMM, and bidirectionality

HMMMEMM
TypeGenerativeDiscriminative

ModelT̂ = argmax Π P(wᵢ|tᵢ) P(tᵢ|tᵢ₋₁)T̂ = argmax Π P(tᵢ | wᵢ, tᵢ₋₁)

The reason to use a discriminative sequence model is that it is much easier to incorporate a lot of features.

Features an MEMM can use and an HMM cannot

Tagging Janet/NNP will/MD back/VB the/DT bill/NN, when wᵢ = back:

wi = back ti-1 = MD ti-2 ti-1 = NNP MD wi-1 = will wi+1 = the wi contains no digit wi is lowercase suffix(wi) = "ack" prefix(wi) = "b"

None of these are expressible in an HMM. Note especially suffix — that is what lets an MEMM tag a word it has never seen.

Decoding an MEMM

Bidirectionality

The problem: HMM taggers as presented run exclusively left-to-right — though Viterbi does let present decisions be influenced indirectly by future ones, since the whole path is optimised.

Two-passFirst pass uses POS features only from already-disambiguated words on the left. Second pass can use tags for all words, including those on the right.
Two-directionRun the tagger twice — once left-to-right, once right-to-left — and in Viterbi decoding pick the higher-scoring sequence.

Modern taggers are generally run bidirectionally. This is exactly what the "Bi-" in Bi-LSTM and the "B" in BERT are for → Chapter 7.

Three problems with HMM tagging (and their fixes): unknown words → smoothing · limited context → trigram/4-gram HMMs · sparsity → smoothing again.
