1 · Why turn a word into numbers at all?

Analogy. Imagine you run a music shop and you file every album by its title string. A customer asks for "something like Pink Floyd". Your filing system is useless — the letters in "Pink Floyd" tell you nothing about which other album is similar. Now imagine instead you filed every album by a few numbers: how loud, how fast, how sad, how long. Suddenly "find me something nearby" is a trivial question. That is the entire move this chapter makes, applied to words instead of albums.

The point. With word identity, only the exact same string fires the feature. With word vectors, a nearby vector still (partly) fires it — so the model generalises to words it never saw in training. That is the whole reason vector semantics exists.

2 · The one idea: you are known by the company you keep

Analogy. You have never met my friend Ravi. But I tell you he is always seen at the gym, always carrying a protein shake, always talking about deadlifts. You now know roughly what kind of person Ravi is — not from a definition, but from the company he keeps. Words work the same way.

The distributional hypothesis — words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. Zellig Harris, 1954.

Window size changes what "similar" even means.
Small window (±2) → neighbours are syntactically similar, same taxonomy. Hogwarts → Sunnydale, Evernight, Blandings (other fictional schools).
Large window (±5) → neighbours are related, same semantic field. Hogwarts → Dumbledore, half-blood, Malfoy (the Harry Potter world).

Exam trap. "Sparse" does not mean "small" — it means mostly zero. A sparse vector is the longer of the two. And sparse is not "bad": TF-IDF vectors are still the standard for information retrieval, because a dimension you can name is a dimension you can search. Dense wins as features for machine learning.

5 · Why dense wins: the car / automobile problem

This is the argument the exam wants written out in full. Click to see it happen.

Two sentences that clearly mean almost the same thing:

A: "I parked the car outside." B: "I parked the automobile outside."
Write this in the exam. In a sparse vector, car and automobile are two distinct, unrelated dimensions. A word whose neighbour is car and a word whose neighbour is automobile get counts in two different slots — so the sparse representation shows zero overlap and fails to see that they are similar. A dense vector has no per-word slot, so both training signals push the same numbers in the same direction and the two words collapse into nearly the same vector.

The other three reasons dense wins

#ReasonIn plain terms
1Fewer weights to tune300 weights per class instead of 50,000. Easier to train, less overfitting.
2Generalise better than explicit countsSimilar words share numbers, so evidence transfers.
3Capture synonymy (above)The one you must be able to argue.
4In practice they simply work betterThe honest empirical answer.
The worst sparse vector of all: one-hot. Length |V|, a single 1, rest zeros. Every pair of one-hot vectors is orthogonal, so the dot product of any two different words is exactly 0 — one-hot says apple and orange are as unrelated as apple and king. It carries no similarity information at all.

Why not just the dot product? The dot product favours long vectors, and a vector is long when its counts are big. Frequent words like the co-occur with everything, so their vectors are enormous and the raw dot product declares the similar to everything. Cosine divides out both lengths, so it measures only the angle — the pattern of co-occurrence rather than its volume.
Reading the numbers. Because raw counts are never negative, cosine on a term–term matrix is confined to 0 … 1. You will never see a negative cosine here — if you do, you made an arithmetic error.

7 · TF-IDF calculator

Analogy. You are told three facts about a stranger: "they breathe", "they own a phone", "they play the bassoon". Only the third one tells you anything, because the first two are true of nearly everyone. TF-IDF is exactly this instinct as arithmetic — a word that appears in every document is told to shut up.
count(t, d) N (total docs) df (docs with t)
Two traps.
1. df counts documents, not occurrences. A word appearing 500 times inside one document has df = 1.
2. A word in every document has idf = log(N/N) = 0, so its tf-idf is 0 no matter how often it occurs. Say that explicitly — it is usually worth a mark.

Static vs contextual — the 2-mark question. word2vec and GloVe are static: one fixed vector per word type, so bank (river) and bank (money) share a single vector. ELMo and BERT are contextual: a distinct embedding for each token, computed from its actual context.
