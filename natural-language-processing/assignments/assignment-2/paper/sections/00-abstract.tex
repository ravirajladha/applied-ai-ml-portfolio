Summarizing product reviews is usually treated as a modelling problem. On real
e-commerce data it is at least equally a problem of \emph{form}. We fine-tune
\texttt{t5-small}, a 60M-parameter encoder--decoder, on 4{,}000 Amazon food
reviews whose references are the four-word headlines customers wrote
themselves, and evaluate on 300 held-out reviews. Fine-tuning raises ROUGE-1
from 0.109 to 0.175. The instructive result is the baseline: the same
pretrained model \emph{without} fine-tuning scores 0.109, below the 0.126 of a
trivial baseline returning the review's first sentence. Its output is fluent
and on topic but the wrong shape, and we argue that fine-tuning on such
references buys register rather than domain knowledge --- which also constrains
what our own gain demonstrates. We then show that per-review summarization
conceals aggregate signal: for a baby formula with a 3.13 star average and
predominantly positive summaries, contrastive aspect mining surfaces sustained
complaints about arsenic levels and infant health. Star averages, per-review
summaries and summaries-of-summaries are all averaging operations, and a
minority complaint survives none of them.
