\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A popular product on an e-commerce platform accumulates thousands of reviews.
No buyer reads them. The star average, the one summary statistic that is
always shown, compresses every one of those opinions into a single number and
discards what the reviews actually said.

Automatic summarization is the obvious remedy, and it is usually posed as a
single problem: given a document, produce a shorter document. Product reviews
split it into two. The first is per-review compression --- turning one review
into a line a shopper can scan. The second is aggregation --- saying what
customers as a group praise and criticise, which no per-review summarizer can
answer, because the answer is not contained in any individual review. We treat
both, and find that the second is where the interesting information lives.

We fine-tune \texttt{t5-small} \cite{raffel2020t5}, a 60M-parameter
encoder--decoder, on 4{,}000 reviews from the Amazon Fine Food Reviews corpus
\cite{mcauley2013amazon}, using the headline each customer wrote as the
reference summary, and evaluate on 300 held-out reviews with ROUGE
\cite{lin2004rouge}. Fine-tuning raises ROUGE-1 from 0.109 to 0.175, a relative
improvement of 61\%.

The headline number is not the interesting one. The interesting one is the
baseline it is measured against. A trivial extractive baseline that returns the
review's first sentence and nothing else scores 0.126 --- and the
\emph{pretrained model without fine-tuning} scores 0.109, below it. A
60M-parameter model trained on a large corpus loses to a one-line heuristic.

It does not lose because it misunderstands the reviews. Its outputs are fluent,
on-topic and recognisably summaries. It loses because it writes full sentences
where the reference is a four-word headline: pretraining taught it what a
summary looks like in general, and this task rewards a specific and unusual
shape. What fine-tuning buys here is form, not knowledge.
Section~\ref{sec:analysis} develops the argument and sets out what it costs us
--- if the metric largely rewards conformity of form, then our own improvement
shows the model learned the target register, not that a reader would prefer its
output.

The second finding concerns what per-review summarization conceals. For a baby
formula product with a 3.13 star average, the generated per-review summaries
read as broadly positive. Aspect mining over the same reviews returns a
criticism list headed by \emph{arsenic levels}, \emph{health} and
\emph{constipation}. The complaint is present in the reviews, survives into
several individual generated summaries, and is invisible in both the star
average and the overall positive impression the summaries create.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We document a case where a pretrained encoder--decoder scores below a
    trivial extractive baseline, and attribute it to a mismatch of output form
    rather than of content (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
  \item We characterise what ROUGE can and cannot support when references are
    approximately four words long, where the metric becomes coarse enough that
    absolute values are not comparable across benchmarks.
  \item We show that aggregate aspect mining surfaces safety-relevant
    complaints that both the star average and the per-review summaries hide,
    and argue that review summarization should be evaluated at the aggregate
    level rather than only per review.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Scope.} This is a small study and we state its limits at the outset.
Results come from one dataset in one product domain, one model size, a single
seed and a single training epoch on CPU. We report no significance test, and
the from-scratch recurrent baseline we implemented was never trained, so the
paper contains no from-scratch comparison. Section~\ref{sec:limitations}
returns to each of these. The claims we make are correspondingly narrow: we
describe a failure mode and an evaluation hazard, not a new method.
