\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

We use the Amazon Fine Food Reviews corpus \cite{mcauley2013amazon}, 568{,}454
reviews of grocery products. The corpus is attractive for supervised
summarization for a practical reason: alongside the review body, every row
carries the short headline the customer wrote themselves. That headline serves
as a reference summary, so the dataset supplies hundreds of thousands of
document--summary pairs at no annotation cost.

That convenience comes with a condition that shapes everything in this paper.
Table~\ref{tab:lengths} gives the length distribution.

\textbf{The reference is a headline, not an abstract.} At a mean of 4.1 words
and a median of 4, the target is roughly one twentieth the length of the input.
This is not the regime that summarization benchmarks are usually built for:
news corpora pair documents with multi-sentence abstracts. The consequence for
evaluation is severe enough that we return to it in
Section~\ref{sec:analysis}: a four-word reference contains only three bigrams,
so ROUGE-2 on a single example can take just four distinct values.

A second property of the corpus is a strong positivity skew --- 78.1\% of
reviews carry four or five stars. We do not correct for this. It is a genuine
property of the deployment setting, and it is precisely what makes the
aggregate analysis of Section~\ref{sec:analysis} interesting: when almost
everything is positive, the minority signal is easy to lose.

\subsection{Filtering}

A row is kept only if the review body is between 50 and 2{,}000 characters and
the headline is between 10 and 80 characters. Both bounds remove pairs that
carry no summarization task rather than pairs that are merely inconvenient. A
30-character review such as \emph{``Great product, arrived fast.''} paired with
the headline \emph{``Great product!''} asks the model to copy, not to compress;
training on such pairs teaches copying. At the other end, a headline longer
than 80 characters is no longer a headline. The filter retains \textbf{84.6\%}
of rows.

\subsection{Splits}

From the filtered pool we draw 4{,}000 training, 300 validation and 500 test
reviews with seed 42. All reported results are computed on \textbf{300} of the
held-out test reviews, none of which the model saw during training. We note that during development, a check on only 24 reviews returned a ROUGE-1
of approximately 0.23 where the full 300 returned approximately 0.17 --- the
small sample was optimistic by roughly a third, which is why it was abandoned.
Both figures come from an earlier run than the results in
Section~\ref{sec:results} and we quote them only to the precision that
comparison supports.
