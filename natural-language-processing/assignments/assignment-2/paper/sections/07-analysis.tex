\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Fluency is not task fit}

The un-tuned model's outputs are not incoherent. They are grammatical, on
topic, and would be recognised by any reader as summaries of the review they
came from. They are also, in this evaluation, worse than returning the first
sentence unchanged.

The explanation is not in the content but in the shape. Pretraining on a large
and varied corpus teaches a model what a summary looks like in general, and in
general a summary is a sentence. Our references are what a customer types into
a one-line form: a mean of 4.1 words, frequently a fragment, often with the
grammatical subject omitted. A model producing a well-formed sentence of
fifteen words has, by construction, fifteen chances to introduce a token the
four-word reference does not contain, and only four chances to hit one it does.
Precision collapses. The first sentence of the review, by contrast, is drawn
from the same document and the same vocabulary the customer had in mind when
writing their headline, and so overlaps with it more often than fluent
paraphrase does.

What fine-tuning supplies on 4{,}000 examples is not knowledge about food. It
is the register: short, blunt, often verbless. This distinction is easy to lose
in applied work, where a pretrained model that emits plausible output invites
the conclusion that it is working. On this task it was producing the right
content in a form the task does not reward, and no amount of additional
pretraining would have fixed that.

\subsection{What ROUGE can support here, and what it cannot}
\label{sec:rouge-limits}

Section~\ref{sec:data} established that the references average four words. The
consequence for ROUGE-2 is arithmetic: a four-word reference contains three
bigrams, so on a single example ROUGE-2 can take only the values $0$, $0.333$,
$0.667$ and $1$. It is not a fine-grained measurement at this length but a
four-way vote, and the corpus-level figure of 0.0448 is an average over such
votes rather than a value any example took. A reference of paragraph length
contains dozens of bigrams and behaves like a continuous quantity. Reporting
our 0.0448 beside the ROUGE-2 values reported on news benchmarks would compare
two different kinds of object.

This also explains an observation we can only report anecdotally: an early
evaluation on 24 reviews returned ROUGE-1 of 0.234, against 0.174 on the full
300. A coarse per-example metric averaged over a small sample is a
high-variance estimator, and the small sample happened to be flattering. We did
not run this as a controlled sweep, and we do not present it as one.

A reader may reasonably object that if ROUGE is a poor proxy for quality --- as
SummEval \cite{fabbri2021summeval} found, reporting weak correlation between
automatic metrics and human judgement --- then a result expressed entirely in
ROUGE establishes little. We think this objection, taken seriously, does not
undermine the finding of Section~\ref{sec:results} but clarifies it.

Our claim is not that the un-tuned model writes worse summaries than Lead-1.
Judged by a human reader, it may well write better ones: they are fluent,
abstractive, and often capture the review's point, where Lead-1 simply copies a
sentence that may or may not be the relevant one. The claim is that the
un-tuned model scores worse, and that it does so because ROUGE is substantially
measuring conformity to the reference's form. SummEval's result is that ROUGE
and human judgement come apart; our result is a concrete mechanism by which
they come apart on short references. The two are consistent, and the second is
an instance of the first.

The honest cost of accepting this is that it constrains what we may claim about
our own model. If ROUGE largely measures form-fit, then the fine-tuned model's
61.2\% improvement demonstrates that it learned the target register --- not
that a shopper would prefer its summaries. We did not run a human evaluation,
so we cannot speak to the latter. A metric such as BERTScore
\cite{zhang2020bertscore}, which compares contextual embeddings rather than
surface tokens, would be less sensitive to form and would be the natural next
measurement; we did not run it.

\subsection{What per-review summarization hides}

The second finding concerns the aggregate. For the demonstration product, a
baby formula with 60 reviews and a mean rating of 3.13 stars, sentence-level
sentiment classifies 46 reviews positive, 13 negative and 1 neutral, and the
per-review summaries read accordingly: \emph{``great product for baby''},
\emph{``safe for my child''}, \emph{``good formula''}.

The contrastive aspect lists tell a second story. The praised aspects are
unremarkable --- \emph{formula}, \emph{milk}, \emph{earth}, \emph{breast},
\emph{months}. The criticised aspects are \emph{formulas}, \emph{hard},
\emph{health}, \emph{levels}, \emph{constipation}. Inspecting the reviews
behind them, a minority of customers are discussing arsenic contamination, and
the model's own summaries of those individual reviews state it plainly:
\emph{``inorganic arsenic levels equal or higher than fda standard for drinking
water''}.

Every representation a shopper is normally offered conceals this. The star
average of 3.13 conveys mild dissatisfaction of unspecified kind. The majority
of per-review summaries are positive and, read in sequence, produce a positive
impression. Even the reduce step, which compresses the summaries into one
sentence, is a majority-driven operation: it is asked to summarise sixty
summaries of which forty-six are favourable. A minority signal survives none of
these, and it survives none of them for the same reason --- each is an
averaging operation, and the complaint is a minority.

The aspect list survives it precisely because it is not an averaging operation.
Selecting terms by how disproportionately they fall on the negative side, and
not by how often they occur overall, is what allows a complaint made by
thirteen of sixty customers to appear at all. This is the argument for
evaluating and designing review summarizers at the aggregate level: the
information a buyer most needs is, structurally, the information that a
summary of the majority removes. It is also, we note, the case in which getting
it wrong matters most --- the minority report here concerns infant health.

We temper this in one respect. The aspect miner is a frequency-and-polarity
heuristic, and it shows: \emph{formula} heads the praised list while
\emph{formulas} heads the criticised one, because singular and plural forms are
collapsed within each list but not across them. A lemmatiser would resolve it.
The finding does not depend on that defect, but the lists are noisier than they
should be.

\subsection{Error analysis}

The failure in Table~\ref{tab:examples} --- a review praising a dog treat and
then complaining at length about the flatulence it caused, summarised as
\emph{``a great treat!''} --- is worth naming precisely, because it is not the
error that summarization work usually attends to.

Maynez et al. \cite{maynez2020faithfulness} distinguish hallucinations that
misrepresent the source from those introducing content absent from it. This is
neither. Every word of \emph{``a great treat!''} is supported by the review; a
faithfulness metric checking whether each claim is entailed by the source would
pass it. The summary is faithful to a part of the source and unrepresentative
of the whole. What has been lost is not accuracy but proportion.

This failure mode is systematic rather than incidental, and it follows from the
output length. A four-word summary of a mixed review must choose one polarity,
and a model fine-tuned on a corpus in which 78.1\% of reviews are positive has
a clear prior about which to choose. The reviews on which it fails are
therefore exactly the mixed ones --- which are also the reviews a shopper would
most want summarised, since a uniformly positive review carries little
information. It is the same structural problem the
aggregate analysis exposes, reappearing at the level of a single document: a
compression that must pick a side will pick the majority one, and the minority
view is what the reader needed.
