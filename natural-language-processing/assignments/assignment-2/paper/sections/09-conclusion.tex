\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We fine-tuned a 60M-parameter encoder--decoder to summarise product reviews
against references that are four-word customer headlines, and report two
findings.

The first is that the same pretrained model, before fine-tuning, scores below a
baseline that returns the review's first sentence. Its output is fluent and on
topic; it is the wrong shape. What fine-tuning contributes on a task of this
kind is conformity to the target register rather than knowledge of the domain,
and a metric built on surface overlap rewards that conformity heavily. The
practical reading is that a pretrained model producing plausible output on a
new task is not evidence that it fits the task, and the gap between the two is
largest exactly where references are short and stylised --- which describes a
great deal of user-generated text.

The second is that summarizing reviews individually removes the signal a buyer
most needs. Per-review summaries, a star average and a summary-of-summaries are
all averaging operations, and a complaint held by a minority survives none of
them. In our demonstration a sustained concern about arsenic levels in an
infant product was invisible in a 3.13 star average and in a set of
predominantly positive generated summaries, and appeared only in an aspect
analysis that ranks terms by how disproportionately they attach to one
polarity rather than by how often they occur.

We therefore suggest that review summarization systems should be specified and
evaluated at the aggregate level, and that per-review ROUGE, whatever its
value as a development signal, does not measure the thing such a system exists
to do. The most useful next steps on this work are the two measurements we did
not make: training the from-scratch baseline to separate the contribution of
pretraining, and a human or embedding-based evaluation to establish whether
matching the reference register corresponds to being more useful to a reader.
