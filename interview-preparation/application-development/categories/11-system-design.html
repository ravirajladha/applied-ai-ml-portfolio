SECTION 1

How to run a design round

"Help shape technical architecture decisions" appears in most mid-level job descriptions, so expect a design round. The good news: they reward process far more than knowledge, and process can be rehearsed.

PrimerThe framework, and what is actually being marked

A design interview is not testing whether you know the correct architecture. There isn't one. It is testing four things:

  1. Do you clarify before building? Candidates who start drawing immediately fail here before they have said anything technical.
  2. Can you decompose a vague problem into components with responsibilities?
  3. Do you reason about trade-offs, or recite technologies? "I'd use Kafka" is worth nothing. "I'd use a partitioned log here because we need replay and multiple independent consumers, and the cost is operational complexity" is worth everything.
  4. Do you know where your design breaks? Volunteering the weakness is the strongest move available, because a senior engineer is someone who knows what will hurt.

The six steps, with timings for a 45-minute round

StepTimeDo
1. Clarify5 minWho uses it, top three use cases, what is out of scope, what "done" looks like. Write the agreed scope where you both can see it.
2. Estimate5 minUsers, requests per second, data volume and growth, read/write ratio, latency target, retention.
3. API5 minThree or four endpoints or events. Forces the data model to become concrete.
4. Data model5 minEntities, relationships, and which store — with the reason.
5. Architecture15 minThe boxes and arrows. Walk one request through end to end out loud.
6. Attack it10 minBottleneck, single point of failure, 10× traffic, failure modes, security, cost, monitoring.

Three sentences that improve almost any design answer

  • "Before I design anything — who uses this, and what are the two or three things they actually need it to do?"
  • "Let me start with the simplest thing that could work, then find the point where it breaks." (Starting simple and evolving under pressure is much stronger than opening with a microservice diagram.)
  • "The weakness of this design is X, and if we hit that I'd address it by Y."
The most common failure at mid-level: over-engineering. Proposing Kubernetes, event sourcing, CQRS and six services for a system with 200 users. It reads as someone who has watched conference talks rather than run systems. Right-sizing is the senior signal.
Q1Design: an environmental sensor ingestion and alerting platform▼

A very common design question in the infrastructure and environmental space. Air quality, water level, noise, structural strain monitors across many sites.

1. Clarify
  • How many devices, and at what frequency? (Say 50,000 devices, one reading per minute.)
  • Is anything acted on automatically, or is it dashboards and reports?
  • What is the alert latency requirement — seconds, or "within the hour"?
  • How long must raw data be retained? (Regulatory answer: usually years.)
  • Do devices buffer when connectivity drops? (They do — so out-of-order and late data are normal, not exceptional.)
2. Estimate

50,000 devices × 1/min ≈ 833 events/second average, perhaps 2,500 at peak. At ~200 bytes each that is ~14 GB/day raw, ~5 TB/year. Comfortably within one Event Hubs namespace and a lakehouse — not a problem needing exotic scale, and saying so is a mark in your favour.

3. Architecture
Devices ──▶ IoT Hub / Event Hubs ──┬──▶ Stream processor ──▶ Alert rules
           (device identity,        │    (windowed aggregates)      │
            buffering, ordering     │                               ▼
            per device)             │                        Service Bus ──▶ Notification service
                                    │
                                    └──▶ Capture to ADLS (bronze, raw Parquet)
                                                    │
                                          Databricks / ADF (silver: cleaned, gap-filled)
                                                    │
                                          Gold: hourly & daily aggregates
                                                    │
                                   ┌────────────────┴─────────────────┐
                                   ▼                                  ▼
                          Time-series API  ◀── Dashboard        Power BI / reports
4. The decisions to justify out loud
  • Event Hubs, not a queue, because multiple independent consumers need the same stream (alerting, archival, analytics) and replay matters when a downstream consumer has a bug. Partition by device ID for per-device ordering.
  • Capture raw to storage immediately, before any processing. If the transformation is wrong you reprocess; ingestion is the one step you cannot redo.
  • Alerting is separate from analytics — different latency requirements, so different paths. Do not make a dashboard query the alerting path.
  • Downsample for retention: raw for 90 days, hourly for 2 years, daily forever. Otherwise storage grows without bound for data nobody queries at that resolution.
  • Alert state matters more than alert detection. A sensor oscillating around a threshold must not send 400 notifications — you need debouncing, hysteresis, deduplication and acknowledgement. This is the part most candidates miss and it is the part that decides whether people trust the system.
5. Attack it

Late and out-of-order data from buffered devices — use event time with a watermark, and design aggregates to be recomputable. A device with a stuck clock poisoning time-based aggregates — validate timestamps on ingest. Missing data is meaningful: a sensor that stops reporting may be the most important signal, so monitor for silence, not just for values. Backpressure if the processor falls behind — the stream buffers, which is why it is a log rather than a queue.

Q2Design: an offline-first field inspection app▼

Engineers inspecting bridges, culverts and substations, frequently with no signal. This is the design most specific to real field-engineering work, and offline sync is a genuinely hard problem — which makes it a good one to show judgement on.

1. Clarify
  • How long offline — hours, or days?
  • Can two inspectors edit the same asset? (This determines whether you need conflict resolution at all — and if the answer is no, say so and skip the complexity.)
  • Photo and video volume per inspection? This dominates the sync design.
  • Does the inspector need reference data offline (drawings, previous inspections, asset register)?
2. The design
Mobile app
 ├── Local store (SQLite)            asset cache + pending changes queue
 ├── Outbox of operations            each with a client-generated UUID
 └── Sync engine                     runs when connectivity returns
                │
                ▼
        Sync API  (idempotent, batched)
                │
   ┌────────────┼──────────────────┐
   ▼            ▼                  ▼
 Assets DB   Blob storage    Service Bus ──▶ downstream processing
             (photos, direct
              upload via SAS)
3. The decisions that matter
  • Client-generated IDs (UUIDs). The device must create records while offline, so the server cannot be the source of identity. This also makes sync naturally idempotent.
  • Sync an operation log, not final state. "Set condition to 62 at 09:20" merges far better than "here is my whole record".
  • Photos upload separately and resumably, direct to blob storage via a SAS URL, at lower priority than the structured data. A 4G connection at the edge of a field will not survive a 200 MB upload — so structured data syncs first and the inspection is usable before the media arrives.
  • Conflict policy decided by the business, not the code. Last-write-wins is fine for a note and wrong for a safety rating. Ask; then implement the answer, flagging genuine conflicts for human review rather than silently discarding one.
  • Reference data pre-downloaded by area before going on site, with a version so the app can tell the user their cache is stale.
  • The sync endpoint must be idempotent and batched — one call with 40 operations, replayable safely. See category 05, Q5.
4. Attack it

A device syncing after two weeks offline against reference data that has changed — needs a schema/version negotiation and a defined behaviour. Storage limits on the device after many inspections with photos — needs eviction of synced data. Battery and data usage, which is a real constraint for a full day in the field. Security of the local cache — an unencrypted device store containing client site data is a serious exposure, so encrypt at rest and support remote wipe.

Say the meta-point: "Offline sync is one of those problems where the right first question is how much of it we genuinely need. If inspectors are offline for hours rather than days and never edit the same asset, a queue of operations with client-generated IDs is enough, and I'd avoid building a conflict-resolution engine nobody needs."

Q3Design: a document management and search platform▼

Engineering consultancies run on documents — drawings, specifications, reports, standards, correspondence — across thousands of projects, with strict access control and retention.

The design
Upload ──▶ API ──▶ SAS URL ──▶ Blob Storage (versioned, immutable, tiered)
                    │
                    └─▶ Event Grid ──▶ Function (processing pipeline)
                                          ├── virus scan
                                          ├── text + metadata extraction (OCR for scans)
                                          ├── thumbnail generation
                                          └── index into Azure AI Search
                                                   │
Search API ◀───────────────────────────────────────┘
   │  (security trimming applied at query time)
   ▼
 Metadata DB (Azure SQL): project, document type, revision, status, permissions
The decisions
  • Metadata in SQL, bytes in blob storage. Never store files in the database.
  • Immutable versions. A revision is a new blob, never an overwrite. In a discipline where "which revision was issued for construction?" is a legal question, this is a requirement, not a design preference.
  • Processing is asynchronous and event-driven — the upload returns immediately; OCR of a 300-page scan happens behind it. The document has a processing status the UI shows.
  • Security trimming at query time. The search index carries permission identifiers and the query filters on the caller's groups. Filtering results after retrieval leaks existence and breaks paging counts.
  • Lifecycle to cool and archive tiers — most documents are read in their first month and retained for decades.
  • Full-text plus metadata facets, because engineers search by project, discipline and revision at least as often as by words.
The natural extension question: "how would you add a question-answering assistant over this?" You already have the ingestion and permission model — add chunking, embeddings and a vector index, retrieve with the same security trimming, and return citations. See category 10, Q11. Being able to connect the two designs is exactly the hybrid profile this role is looking for.

Q4Design: a notification service▼

A common design question, and a good one because it is deceptively simple.

Producers ──▶ Service Bus topic ──▶ Notification service
                                       ├── resolve recipients + preferences
                                       ├── apply quiet hours / rate caps / dedup
                                       ├── render template (per channel, per locale)
                                       └── dispatch
                                             ├── Email provider
                                             ├── SMS provider
                                             └── Push (mobile)
                                       └── record status; retry; dead-letter
What makes this question interesting
  • Idempotency. At-least-once delivery means the same event may arrive twice. Without a deduplication key, users get duplicate emails — the most visible possible bug.
  • Preferences and quiet hours are the actual product. Sending is easy; not sending the wrong thing at 3am is the feature.
  • Templates are versioned content, not code, so they can change without a deployment — and need a preview and approval path.
  • Third-party providers fail. Retry with backoff, circuit-break, and consider a secondary provider for critical channels.
  • Priority separation. A critical safety alert must not queue behind 50,000 weekly digest emails — use separate queues or priority handling.
  • Delivery status matters: sent, delivered, bounced, opened. Bounces must feed back or you keep sending to a dead address and damage your sending reputation.
The trap: putting the send in the request path of whatever triggered it. If the email provider is slow, saving an inspection becomes slow. Always decouple through a queue — this is the clearest everyday example of when to go asynchronous.

Q5Design: an asset dashboard with geospatial filtering▼

A map with tens of thousands of assets, filterable by type, condition and area, with a detail panel.

The problems, in order of how often they are missed
  • Do not send 50,000 features to the browser. Query by the current map viewport (bounding box), and cluster or aggregate at low zoom — at national zoom the user cannot distinguish individual points anyway. Either server-side clustering or pre-generated vector tiles.
  • Spatial index on the geometry column, or every pan is a full table scan.
  • Separate the list from the detail. Return minimal fields for the map (id, position, status colour) and fetch the full record only when something is selected.
  • Cache aggressively. Asset positions change rarely; condition changes daily. Cache the geometry layer hard, the status layer briefly — two different cache policies for two different data lifetimes.
  • Debounce map movement so a pan does not fire thirty queries.
  • Coordinate reference system agreed end to end — see category 09, Q16. Mixing a projected CRS with WGS84 puts assets in the wrong place, subtly enough that nobody notices for a week.
The performance framing: "The bottleneck on a map view is almost never the database — it's payload size and the number of DOM or canvas objects. So I'd design the API around what the viewport actually needs rather than around the entity."

Q6The generic scaling toolkit — reach for these in order▼

When asked "what if traffic grows 10×?", walk this list. Cheapest and least disruptive first — the order itself is the answer.

StepLever
1Measure. Find the actual bottleneck. Everything below is guessing until you have.
2Fix the obvious query problems — indexes, N+1, missing pagination. Usually the biggest single win, and free.
3Cache — HTTP caching, then a distributed cache for hot reads.
4Scale out the stateless tier. Easy, provided the application is genuinely stateless.
5Move work out of the request path onto a queue. Often the largest structural improvement available.
6Read replicas for read-heavy workloads — accepting replication lag.
7CDN for static and cacheable content.
8Split the hot component into its own service so it scales independently.
9Partition / shard the data. Powerful and hard to reverse — last resort.
Jumping to step 9 is the classic mid-level error. Sharding a database that would be fine with an index is the architectural equivalent of demolishing a house to fix a dripping tap. Walking the list in order demonstrates judgement rather than knowledge.

Q7How do you talk while designing?▼

The mechanics matter, especially remotely.

  • Narrate your reasoning, not your conclusions. "I'm putting a queue here because I don't want report generation in the request path" beats silently drawing a queue.
  • Check in. "Does that scope sound right before I go further?" every ten minutes. Interviewers usually want to steer you toward what they intend to probe, and you should let them.
  • Say what you are deferring. "I'll assume authentication is handled by Entra ID and come back to authorisation detail if we have time." Shows you know it exists without burning the clock.
  • Draw badly and quickly. Boxes and arrows. Nobody is marking your handwriting; on a video call, paper held up to the camera is completely normal.
  • When you do not know, say so and reason anyway. "I haven't used Cosmos at that scale — the thing I'd need to establish first is the partition key, because it's the decision you can't undo." That is a better answer than confident invention, and interviewers are explicitly listening for whether you bluff.
  • Watch the clock and cover the whole problem. A complete design with shallow detail scores better than a perfect authentication layer and no architecture.

Q8The trade-offs you should be able to argue both sides of▼

Interviewers probe by pushing back. Be ready to defend and to concede.

Trade-offThe pivot
Consistency vs availabilityWhat is the cost of a stale read for this specific data? A dashboard count, fine. A permission check, no.
Monolith vs microservicesTeam structure and release cadence, not code size.
Sync vs asyncDoes the caller need the answer to continue?
SQL vs NoSQLDo relationships and transactions matter, or scale and flexible shape?
Normalise vs denormaliseWrite correctness vs read speed. Denormalise deliberately and own the consistency.
Build vs buyIs this our differentiator? Nobody wins by building their own identity provider.
Cache vs freshnessHow stale can this be before someone makes a wrong decision?
Batch vs real-timeWhat decision does it drive, and how fast must that decision be made?
The universal move when pushed: "That's a fair objection — it depends on X. If X is true, your approach is better; if not, I'd stay with mine because of Y." That shows you hold a position for reasons rather than by habit, and it is what they are probing for. Changing your mind under a good argument is a strength; changing it under mild pressure is not.
