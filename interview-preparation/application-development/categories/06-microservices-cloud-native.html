SECTION 1

Boundaries, communication and data

The architectural half. This is where an interviewer decides whether you have opinions or vocabulary.

PrimerWhat you are actually buying, and what it costs

Microservices are not a better way to write software. They are a trade: you accept a large amount of operational and distributed-systems complexity in exchange for organisational benefits. Understanding it as a trade rather than as progress is the difference between a mid-level and a senior answer.

You gainYou pay
Independent deployment — one team ships without coordinating a releaseEvery call can now fail, be slow, or arrive twice
Independent scaling — scale only the hot componentNo more database transactions across the whole operation
Technology choice per serviceDebugging spans several codebases and needs distributed tracing
Fault isolation — one service dying does not take everythingMore infrastructure, more pipelines, more monitoring, more cost
Smaller codebases people can hold in their headsEventual consistency you have to explain to non-technical stakeholders

Conway's law, and why it decides your architecture

Systems tend to mirror the communication structure of the organisation that builds them. Practically: if one team owns everything, splitting into six services just gives that team six deployment pipelines and no independence. Service boundaries that do not match team boundaries produce all of the cost and none of the benefit.

Where the boundaries actually go

Not by technical layer (a "database service" and a "logic service" is just a distributed monolith). By business capability — the domain-driven design term is a bounded context. Signs you have drawn a good boundary:

  • The service owns its data and nothing else reads its tables directly.
  • Most changes to one business capability touch exactly one service.
  • It can be deployed without deploying anything else.
  • It has a clear owner.

The strongest signal of a bad boundary: a single user story routinely requires coordinated changes to three services. That means you split along the wrong seam.

Positioning this honestly if you have not run a large estate

"I haven't operated a large microservice estate. What I've worked with is containerised services and the failure modes between them, and what I'd bring is a strong bias toward starting with a well-modularised monolith and extracting services when there's a concrete reason — a different scaling profile, a different release cadence, or a different team. I've done enough distributed debugging to be wary of splitting early."

That is honest, defensible, and happens to be the position most experienced architects hold — which is why stating it plainly costs nothing and gains credibility.

Q1When would you NOT use microservices?▼

Interviewers ask the negative because everyone can recite the positive.

  • A small team. Under roughly ten engineers, the coordination benefit does not exist and the overhead is pure cost.
  • An unclear domain. Boundaries drawn before you understand the domain will be wrong, and moving a boundary between services is far harder than moving one between modules.
  • Strong transactional requirements across what would be several services. Sagas are real work and real complexity.
  • No operational maturity. Without CI/CD, centralised logging, tracing and on-call, microservices produce outages you cannot diagnose.
  • A greenfield project with an unproven idea. Optimise for changing your mind.
"The default I'd argue for is a modular monolith — one deployable, but with internal module boundaries enforced in the build. You get most of the design benefit, you keep transactions and in-process calls, and every module is a service you can extract later once you know which one actually needs it."

Q2Why can't services share a database?▼

Because a shared schema is a shared, invisible contract. If two services read the same table, neither can change it — you have coupled them at the tightest possible level while pretending they are independent. That is the distributed monolith: all the operational cost of microservices with none of the independence.

Each service owns its data and exposes it only through its API or its events. If service B needs data from A, it either asks A, or it keeps its own read-optimised copy updated from A's events.

The obvious objection, and the answer: "then how do I join across services for a report?" You do not. Options: an aggregating query in the calling service (fine for small sets), a read model maintained from events, or — most often correct — a separate reporting database or data warehouse fed from all services. Reporting and transactional needs genuinely are different problems, and saying so is the mature answer.

Q3Synchronous or event-driven — how do you decide?▼
Synchronous (HTTP/gRPC)Asynchronous (messages/events)
Use whenThe caller needs the answer to proceedThe caller only needs the work to eventually happen
CouplingTemporal — both must be up at the same timeDecoupled — the consumer can be down
FailurePropagates immediately up the chainAbsorbed by the broker; retried later
Load spikesCascadeBuffered by the queue
ConsistencyImmediateEventual
DebuggingStraightforwardNeeds correlation IDs and tracing
The failure mode of synchronous chains: if A calls B calls C and each is 99.9% available, the combined path is about 99.7% — and if C is slow, A's threads are all blocked waiting, so A dies too. Long synchronous chains are the most common cause of cascading failure in microservice systems, which is exactly why timeouts and circuit breakers are mandatory rather than optional.

Q4Events vs commands, and choreography vs orchestration▼

A command tells one specific service to do something (GenerateReport). An event announces that something happened, with no knowledge of who cares (InspectionRecorded). Events are past tense; that naming convention is not cosmetic — it reflects that the publisher does not depend on the consumers.

ChoreographyOrchestration
HowEach service reacts to events; no central controllerOne coordinator tells each service what to do next
GoodLoosely coupled; easy to add a new consumerThe whole flow is visible and debuggable in one place
BadNobody knows what the overall process is — it is emergent, and tracing it means reading six codebasesThe coordinator becomes a bottleneck and a coupling point
The practical answer: choreography for simple fan-out ("an inspection was recorded" → notify, index, recompute); orchestration for a business process with ordering, compensation and a status a user asks about. Most real systems use both, and choosing per flow rather than per architecture is the right instinct.

Q5How do you do a transaction across services? (Saga)▼

You cannot — there is no distributed ACID transaction across independent databases (two- phase commit exists, but it locks resources across services and is avoided for good reason). Instead you use a saga: a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensating action that semantically undoes it.

Book inspection slot        →  ok
Reserve inspector           →  ok
Allocate equipment          →  FAILS
    ↓ compensate, in reverse
Release inspector
Cancel inspection slot

Compensation is not a rollback — the intermediate states were real and may have been visible to users. That is a business conversation, not only a technical one: "the slot was briefly booked and then released" has to be acceptable, or the design is wrong.

Say this: "Sagas are real complexity, so the first question I'd ask is whether these steps belong in the same service. Needing a saga is often evidence that the boundary was drawn in the wrong place."
advancednew
Q6The outbox pattern — the dual-write problem▼

The problem: you save to the database and publish an event. Two systems, no shared transaction. If the publish fails after the commit, the event is lost forever and downstream services are permanently wrong. If you publish first and the commit fails, you announced something that never happened.

The transactional outbox: in the same database transaction, write the business row and an outbox row containing the event. A separate relay process polls the outbox (or reads the database's change feed) and publishes to the broker, marking rows as sent.

BEGIN;
  INSERT INTO inspections (...) VALUES (...);
  INSERT INTO outbox (id, type, payload, created_at)
       VALUES (uuid, 'InspectionRecorded', '{...}', now());
COMMIT;
-- relay publishes from outbox, at least once

This gives at-least-once delivery, so consumers must be idempotent — which is why every event carries an ID.

This is a strong thing to know. Dual writes are one of the most common sources of silent data divergence in event-driven systems, and many candidates at this level have never heard of the outbox. Bringing it up unprompted is memorable.

Q7Message delivery guarantees, ordering and poison messages▼
GuaranteeReality
At most onceMay be lost. Acceptable only for disposable telemetry.
At least onceMay be duplicated. What you almost always get — so consumers must be idempotent.
Exactly onceNot achievable end to end in a distributed system. What is achievable is at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing, which is effectively once. Say it this way — it is a well-known interview discriminator.

Ordering is only guaranteed within a partition or session. If order matters, key the messages by the entity ID (Service Bus sessions, Event Hubs partition key) so all events for one asset go to one consumer in order — at the cost of parallelism for that key.

Poison messages — a message that always fails — must go to a dead-letter queue after N attempts, with an alert. Without that, one bad message blocks the queue forever or spins in an infinite retry loop.

Q8CQRS and event sourcing — know what they are, be cautious about them▼

CQRS separates the write model from the read model. Writes go through the domain model; reads come from a denormalised projection shaped for the screen. Useful when read and write loads or shapes differ sharply.

Event sourcing stores the sequence of events as the source of truth rather than the current state; current state is a fold over the events. You gain a complete audit history and the ability to rebuild any past state or a new projection retroactively.

Show the caution, not just the knowledge. Event sourcing makes simple things hard: querying, deleting personal data on request, and schema evolution of old events you must still be able to read years later. It is excellent for domains that are genuinely event-shaped and audit-heavy — and expensive almost everywhere else. Candidates who enthuse about it without naming a cost are flagged as untested.
Where it fits an asset-heavy domain: asset condition over decades is genuinely an event log — inspections, repairs, incidents — and regulators ask "what did you know and when?" So the domain fits better than most. That is a good, specific observation to make.

Q9Docker: images, layers and a production Dockerfile▼
# ---- build stage: heavy SDK, discarded afterwards ----
FROM mcr.microsoft.com/dotnet/sdk:8.0 AS build
WORKDIR /src
COPY *.csproj .
RUN dotnet restore                       # cached until the csproj changes
COPY . .
RUN dotnet publish -c Release -o /app

# ---- runtime stage: small, no compiler, no source ----
FROM mcr.microsoft.com/dotnet/aspnet:8.0
WORKDIR /app
COPY --from=build /app .
RUN adduser --disabled-password appuser

EXPOSE 8080
ENTRYPOINT ["dotnet", "AssetApi.dll"]
PracticeWhy
Multi-stage buildThe final image has no compiler, no source, no build tools — smaller and a far smaller attack surface
Dependencies before sourceLayer caching: a code change rebuilds one layer, not the dependency install
Non-root userA container escape starts from a much weaker position
Pinned base tags:latest means your build is not reproducible
.dockerignoreKeeps .git, node_modules and local secrets out of the build context

Q10Kubernetes: the objects and the mental model▼

The model: you declare desired state, a control loop reconciles reality toward it, continuously. Everything else follows from that.

ObjectRole
PodOne or more containers sharing a network namespace. Disposable.
ReplicaSetKeeps N pods running. Managed for you by a Deployment.
DeploymentDeclares the image and replica count; performs rolling updates and rollbacks.
ServiceStable virtual IP and DNS name in front of a changing set of pods.
IngressHTTP routing into the cluster, TLS termination.
ConfigMap / SecretConfiguration and credentials as env vars or mounted files.
HPAHorizontal Pod Autoscaler — scales replicas on CPU or a custom metric.
NamespaceLogical isolation with its own quotas and policies.
resources:
  requests: { cpu: "100m", memory: "256Mi" }   # what the scheduler reserves
  limits:   { cpu: "500m", memory: "512Mi" }   # the hard ceiling
readinessProbe: { httpGet: { path: /health/ready, port: 8080 } }
livenessProbe:  { httpGet: { path: /health/live,  port: 8080 } }
Two things that bite in production. Exceeding a memory limit gets the container killed instantly (OOMKilled) with no grace — whereas exceeding a CPU limit merely throttles it. And conflating liveness with readiness: if your liveness probe fails because a database is down, Kubernetes restarts every pod repeatedly and turns a dependency outage into a total outage. Liveness = "am I broken?"; readiness = "should I get traffic right now?"

Q11Graceful shutdown — the detail that separates people▼

When the platform stops a container it sends SIGTERM, waits a grace period, then SIGKILL. A well-behaved service:

  1. Marks readiness as false, so the load balancer stops sending new requests.
  2. Finishes in-flight requests (with a deadline).
  3. Stops consuming from queues and lets current messages complete or return to the queue.
  4. Closes database connections and flushes logs.
  5. Exits.

Without this, every deployment drops a handful of requests. It looks like random intermittent errors during releases, which is a miserable thing to debug — and it is surprisingly common.

Coming from test automation → if you have ever seen a test suite fail intermittently only during a deployment window, this is very often why. Good story.
advancednew
Q12Resilience: timeout, retry, circuit breaker, bulkhead — with the details▼
PatternDetail that matters
TimeoutEvery outbound call. Your timeout must be shorter than your caller's, or you hold their thread past their own deadline. Budget them down the chain.
RetryExponential backoff with jitter. Without jitter, every client retries at the same instant and re-kills the recovering service — the thundering herd. Only retry idempotent operations and only on transient errors (timeouts, 502/503/429), never on a 400.
Circuit breakerClosed → (N failures) → Open, fail fast without calling → (after a cooldown) → Half-open, allow a trial request → Closed or Open again. Stops you wasting resources on a dependency that is down and gives it room to recover.
BulkheadSeparate connection or thread pools per dependency, so one slow downstream cannot consume every thread and take out the unrelated parts of your service.
FallbackCached value, default, or a degraded feature. Decide it deliberately per call.
Load sheddingUnder overload, reject early with 503 rather than accepting work you cannot finish. Slow failure is worse than fast failure.
The compound trap: retries at three layers multiply. Three retries at each of three levels is 27 requests from one user action. Retry at one layer, deliberately chosen — usually the outermost one that understands idempotency.
coreadvanced
Q13How do you debug a problem across five services?▼

You cannot, unless you built for it in advance. That framing is the answer.

  • Correlation ID generated at the edge, propagated in a header through every synchronous call and onto every message, and included in every log line.
  • Distributed tracing (OpenTelemetry → Application Insights): each service records spans with parent-child links, so you get one waterfall showing where the time went and where the error started.
  • Centralised structured logs — searching across services, not SSHing to instances that no longer exist.
  • RED metrics per service: Rate, Errors, Duration. Enough to answer "which service is unhealthy" in seconds.
  • A service dependency map, so you know what "downstream" even means at 2am.
The line to use: "In a monolith you can attach a debugger. In a distributed system observability isn't a nice-to-have, it's the replacement for the debugger — so it has to be built in from the first service, not added after the first bad incident."

Q14How do you test microservices without a nightmare end-to-end suite?▼

This is your subject, and a place to be visibly expert.

LevelWhat it does
UnitDomain logic in isolation. Fast, plentiful.
ComponentOne service end to end in-process, with its real database (Testcontainers) and its collaborators stubbed. High value per test.
ContractConsumer publishes its expectations; provider's build verifies them (Pact). This is what removes the need for a big integration suite and lets services deploy independently.
End-to-endA handful of critical journeys in a shared environment. Expensive, slow, flaky — deliberately few.
In productionSynthetic monitoring, health checks, canary analysis. In a distributed system you cannot test everything before release, so you also test after.
Lead with the argument, not the tool list: "The failure mode I've seen most is teams trying to hold quality with a big end-to-end suite against a shared environment. It's slow, it's flaky, and the environment is broken more often than not — so it becomes a deployment bottleneck and then it gets ignored. Contract tests move that assurance into each service's own pipeline." You can defend every sentence of that from experience, and it is exactly the contribution this role's "contribute to testing practices" line is asking for.

Q17"We have a legacy monolith. How do you modernise it?"▼

Very likely at a consultancy — much of the work is modernising something that exists. The answer is the strangler fig pattern, and the discipline is not doing a rewrite.

  1. Put a facade in front (API gateway or reverse proxy) so traffic can be routed per route without clients knowing.
  2. Pick the first slice by value and risk, not by what is technically neatest — something that changes often, or is a scaling bottleneck, and is reasonably self-contained.
  3. Build the new service alongside, route that slice to it, keep the old code running until traffic is fully migrated.
  4. Migrate the data for that slice, often with a period of dual-writing and reconciliation.
  5. Delete the old code. The step teams skip, which is how you end up maintaining both forever.
  6. Repeat. The monolith shrinks; there is never a big-bang cutover.
Say why not a rewrite: a full rewrite means a long period with no delivered value, and the old system keeps changing while you build, so you are chasing a moving target. Strangler delivers value incrementally and is reversible at every step. Having a clear opinion here is worth a lot in a consulting interview.
